# Module 002 — Lean Business Modeling & Unit Economics

> **Stage:** IDEA→PRE-SEED · **Pillar:** Strategy / Economics · **Prereq:** mod-001

Discovery told you a problem is real. A **business model** is your bet
on *how the company that solves it makes money and survives*: who it's
for, how they find you, what you charge, what it costs to deliver, and
what one customer is worth compared to what one customer costs. Every
downstream module — runway (mod-003), fundraising (mod-004), sales
(mod-006) — reduces to numbers and claims that live on the two artifacts
this module owns.

A model is not a plan; it's an **explicit set of assumptions you can
test**. The canvas exists so the assumptions are visible, one page, and
small enough to attack one at a time. Unit economics exists so that when
you scale, you scale a business, not a subsidy. Deep pricing / packaging
/ channel-economics craft — as a permanent go-to-market discipline — is
deferred to the `startup-product-gtm-curriculum` peer track (see
[chapter 07](./07-boundary-gtm-pricing-channels.md)).

## Learning objectives

After this module you can:

1. Turn discovery findings into a **lean canvas** (Ash Maurya,
   *Running Lean*) — nine blocks filled problem-first, each block
   traceable to evidence rather than opinion.
2. **Distinguish the Lean Canvas from the Business Model Canvas**
   (Osterwalder, *Business Model Generation*) and defend when each is
   appropriate.
3. Build a first **unit-economics sheet** naming and defending the four
   core numbers — CAC, ARPA/ARPU, gross/contribution margin, churn —
   with the **founder-time trap in CAC** called out.
4. Derive **LTV, LTV : CAC ratio, and CAC payback period**, applying
   the SaaS heuristics (David Skok / *For Entrepreneurs*:
   LTV : CAC > 3× as a scale-signal, payback < ~12 months) as
   heuristics rather than laws.
5. Identify the **single riskiest assumption** in the model — the
   load-bearing bet that, if wrong, kills the business — and design the
   **cheapest experiment** that would tell you whether it holds.
6. **Size the market bottom-up** (`segment members × plausible ARPA`)
   as a sanity check, and defend why top-down analyst reports are
   decorative at this stage.
7. Locate the **boundary to `startup-product-gtm-curriculum`** (level
   30) — this module owns the first canvas; the peer track owns
   pricing / packaging / channel-economics as a permanent GTM
   discipline.

## Exercises

The exercises are designed as a progression: build the canvas and
unit-economics sheet, then identify and design an experiment for the
riskiest assumption, then sanity-check the whole thing against a
bottom-up market size.

| # | Exercise | Time | Reads with chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | [Canvas & Unit Economics](./exercises/exercise-01-canvas-and-unit-economics.md) | ~6–8 hr | 01–04 |
| 02 | [Riskiest Assumption Experiment Design](./exercises/exercise-02-riskiest-assumption-experiment-design.md) | ~2 hr | 05 |
| 03 | [Bottom-Up Market Sizing Drill](./exercises/exercise-03-bottom-up-market-sizing-drill.md) | ~90 min | 06 |

Do them in numerical order — exercise 02 depends on the canvas from
exercise 01, and exercise 03 uses the segment and ARPA choices you
made in exercise 01.

## Live-lab note

If you're an operating founder using this as a live lab: do
[exercise 01](./exercises/exercise-01-canvas-and-unit-economics.md) on
*your actual business* this week. Fill the canvas from your own
discovery notes, build the economics sheet with your real (or
best-guess) numbers, and end with the assumption you're most afraid
to test. That last one is usually the one worth testing next.
