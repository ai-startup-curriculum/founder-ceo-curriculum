# Chapter 01 — Lean Canvas: Filling the Nine Blocks Problem-First

> **Reads with:** module objective 1 — *turn discovery findings into a lean
> canvas (Ash Maurya, `Running Lean`) — nine blocks filled problem-first,
> each block traceable to evidence rather than opinion.*

## Why this comes first

A business model is not a business plan. A plan describes what you intend
to do; a **model is an explicit set of assumptions about how the business
works** — who it's for, what pain you relieve, how they find you, what
you charge, what it costs to deliver, and what one customer is worth
compared to what one customer costs. Everything downstream in the founder
curriculum — the operating model (mod-003), the round you raise
(mod-004), the sales motion you build (mod-006) — reduces to numbers and
claims that live on this one page.

The **lean canvas** (Ash Maurya, *Running Lean*, O'Reilly; 3rd ed. 2022)
is the one-page format for that model at the IDEA→PRE-SEED stage. It
adapts Alexander Osterwalder's **Business Model Canvas** (*Business Model
Generation*, 2010) for a company that does not yet have customers,
partners, or key activities to describe. Chapter 02 covers *when to use
which*; this chapter covers *how to fill the lean canvas so it survives
contact with a skeptic*.

## The nine blocks

The lean canvas has nine blocks laid out on one page:

1. **Customer Segments** — who exactly, with an **early adopter** carve-out.
2. **Problem** — the top 1–3 problems this segment has, plus **existing
   alternatives** (competitors, spreadsheets, doing nothing).
3. **Unique Value Proposition (UVP)** — the single-sentence promise.
4. **Solution** — the smallest thing that would deliver the UVP, mapped
   1:1 to the problems.
5. **Channels** — how you reach the segment.
6. **Revenue Streams** — pricing model, price point, willingness-to-pay
   signal.
7. **Cost Structure** — fixed and variable costs; customer acquisition
   cost drivers.
8. **Key Metrics** — the small set of numbers that tell you the model is
   working *now*.
9. **Unfair Advantage** — something a competitor cannot easily copy or
   buy.

Four of these blocks (Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage)
are the ones Maurya swapped in for the Business Model Canvas's Key
Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, and Customer Relationships. Why
that swap is right for pre-PMF startups is the subject of chapter 02.

## Fill order: problem-first, not left-to-right

The canvas is *laid out* left-to-right; it is *filled* problem-first. The
Ash Maurya recommended order:

**Segment → Problem → UVP → Solution → Channels → Revenue → Cost → Key
Metrics → Unfair Advantage.**

The order matters because each downstream block only makes sense once the
upstream blocks have committed to a specific answer. A UVP written before
the problem is a slogan. A pricing model written before the segment is a
guess about a stranger's wallet. Filling the boxes in reading order
almost guarantees that the founder's opinion about the solution smuggles
itself into every downstream block, and the canvas turns into a
one-page pitch instead of a one-page bet.

### Block-by-block, filled from discovery

**1. Customer Segments.** Take the segment definition you narrowed in
mod-001 (`04-sizing-and-segmenting`). Two lines: an **early adopter**
carve-out and a **broader segment**. The early adopter is the sharpest
version of the buyer you have discovery evidence for — not the biggest
market, the *loudest* one. "Seed-stage two-person technical teams who
ship weekly" beats "startups."

**2. Problem.** The top 1–3 problems the segment told you about — ripped
directly from your insight memo (mod-001, chapter 05), counted patterns,
in the buyer's language, not yours. Add a line for **existing
alternatives**: what they're using today. Spreadsheets, a specific
competitor, a manual workaround, or "nothing" are all legitimate
answers; "nothing" is often the most dangerous because it means the pain
isn't loud enough to have prompted a workaround yet.

**3. Unique Value Proposition.** One sentence. It should sound specific,
be aimed at the segment named in block 1, and describe the *finished-story
outcome* — not a feature list. Maurya suggests a **high-concept pitch**
line too ("X for Y" — *"Heroku for machine-learning models"*): a compact
comparison that borrows a mental model your buyer already has. The UVP
test: read it to someone in the segment cold and watch whether they lean
in.

**4. Solution.** The smallest thing that would deliver the UVP. One line
per problem, mapped 1:1 to the problems in block 2. If a solution line
does not correspond to a problem line, one of the two blocks is wrong.
Resist the urge to describe the entire product roadmap — a solution
block full of "planned features" is a signal you're writing a plan, not
a canvas.

**5. Channels.** How the segment finds you. Split into **free / owned**
(content, community, product-led), **paid** (search, social, sponsorship),
and **founder-driven** (warm intros, cold outbound, events). Founders
under-invest here; a great product on a broken channel is a dead product.
If you cannot name at least one channel with a testable cost to reach
the segment, the model is missing a load-bearing block.

**6. Revenue Streams.** Pricing model (subscription, transactional,
usage-based, license), price point (an actual number or a range you'll
defend), and a **willingness-to-pay signal** from discovery: prior spend
on alternatives, a stated budget, a competitor's public price. A canvas
that says "we'll figure out pricing later" is one that hasn't yet made
its most consequential model decision.

**7. Cost Structure.** Two lines: **fixed** (payroll, tooling, base
infra) and **variable per customer** (hosting, per-seat SaaS you resell,
payment fees, third-party APIs, per-request inference cost for AI
products). Third line: **customer acquisition cost drivers** — the ad,
content, or sales-time inputs that scale with new-customer volume. This
block is the input to the unit-economics sheet in chapter 03.

**8. Key Metrics.** Three to five numbers that tell you *right now* the
model is working. The right metrics change with stage: at IDEA it's
"discovery signal count" and "landing-page conversion"; at PRE-SEED it's
"design-partner activations" and "first-value time"; at SEED it's
retention and gross margin. Pick the metrics that would move first if
the model were right, not the ones that would move eventually. Dave
McClure's "AARRR" — acquisition, activation, retention, referral,
revenue — is a durable menu to pick from.

**9. Unfair Advantage.** Something a competitor can't easily copy or
buy. Networks, proprietary data, hard-won regulatory positions,
exclusive distribution, a founding team's specific credential. *Not*
"we work harder," *not* "our team is great," *not* "we're first." If
this box is honestly empty right now, write it empty — that is a
finding, not a failure of the exercise, and it means competitive
strategy needs to be a first-class topic in the next planning cycle.

## Traceability: each block cites its evidence

The most important discipline is the one that isn't in Maurya's original
template: **for every block, write a one-line note naming the discovery
interview, prior-spend signal, competitor teardown, or public data
point that supports the claim.** Blocks with no source are labelled
**ASSUMPTION**. That is fine — a canvas with five assumptions and four
evidence-backed blocks is still useful as long as you know which is
which. What kills a founder is a canvas where every block feels
supported and none of them actually is.

The traceability note also drives the riskiest-assumption exercise in
chapter 05. You can't name the load-bearing bet if you can't tell
opinion from evidence.

## The one-page test

The lean canvas is designed to fit on one page. If yours doesn't, one
of two things is happening:

- **You're describing, not choosing.** Multiple UVPs, multiple
  segments, "and also…" in every block. The canvas is a commitment
  device; it forces you to pick one bet, not survey a landscape.
- **You've collapsed strategy into wish-list mode.** The Solution
  block turned into a feature roadmap; the Channels block became "SEO
  + content + partnerships + paid + PR." Each of those is a real
  option; naming them all as if they were live means none of them is.

The physical constraint is doing real work. Respect it.

## Worked example (from the module exemplar)

Applying the fill order to the mod-001 exemplar segment — *first-time
technical founders in their first 12 months of raising* — the canvas
starts to look like:

- **Segment:** first-time technical founders who incorporated and
  started raising in the last 12 months (early adopter); first-time
  technical founders more broadly (segment).
- **Problem:** simultaneous, unordered exposure to fundraising,
  corporate law, equity, and GTM decisions with no stage-appropriate
  map. Existing alternatives: YC Startup School, scattered essays,
  ad-hoc advisor time.
- **UVP:** a stage-ordered curriculum that tells first-time founders
  what to learn now and what to ignore until later.
- **Solution:** dependency-ordered modules per role; per-module
  "cite the boundary" chapters so the founder knows where their job
  ends.
- **Channels:** open-source repos indexed by search; warm intros
  through accelerator alumni networks; sponsored placement in
  founder-onboarding stacks.
- **Revenue:** free repos; paid cohort / assessment tier at $X/mo.
  Willingness-to-pay: discovery buyers have already spent on YC-adjacent
  courses and 1:1 advisors.
- **Cost:** fixed (author payroll, tooling); variable (LLM inference
  per assessment, cohort-facilitator time).
- **Key metrics:** first-week completion rate, cohort-to-paid
  conversion, per-cohort retention at week 8.
- **Unfair advantage:** structured curriculum with cross-track
  boundary contracts — hard to replicate without the multi-role scope.

Each block would carry a citation note: which interview, which
competitor teardown, which public data point. The point isn't that
this canvas is right — it's that a skeptical reader can see *what
would have to be wrong for it to be wrong*.

## Summary

- The lean canvas is a one-page **model**, not a plan: nine blocks of
  explicit assumptions you can attack one at a time.
- Fill it **problem-first** in Maurya's order — Segment, Problem, UVP,
  Solution, Channels, Revenue, Cost, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage.
- Every block gets a **one-line evidence note** or the label
  **ASSUMPTION**. Both are legitimate; conflating them is not.
- If it doesn't fit on one page, you're describing instead of
  choosing.
