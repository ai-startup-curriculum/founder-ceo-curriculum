# Chapter 02 — Lean Canvas vs Business Model Canvas: When Each Is Right

> **Reads with:** module objective 2 — *distinguish the Lean Canvas from the
> Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder, `Business Model Generation`) and
> defend when each is appropriate.*

## Two canvases, one shared shape

Both the **Business Model Canvas (BMC)** — Alexander Osterwalder and
Yves Pigneur, *Business Model Generation* (Wiley, 2010) — and the **Lean
Canvas** — Ash Maurya, *Running Lean* (O'Reilly; 3rd ed. 2022) — are
one-page, nine-block descriptions of how a company makes money. They
share the same physical layout, the same "everything on one page"
discipline, and the same four blocks on the right-hand side (Customer
Segments, Channels, Customer Relationships / Key Metrics on Lean,
Revenue Streams).

What Maurya changed is the **left-hand side**. Where the BMC has Key
Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, and Customer Relationships,
the Lean Canvas has **Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, and Unfair
Advantage**. Same page, same discipline; different four questions.

## The swap, block by block

| BMC block (Osterwalder) | Lean Canvas block (Maurya) | Why swapped |
|---|---|---|
| Key Partners | **Problem** | A pre-PMF startup has no partners yet; it has a problem it thinks is real. The load-bearing bet is which one, not who's helping. |
| Key Activities | **Solution** | "Activities" presumes you already know what the company does. At the IDEA stage, the *shape* of the solution is the bet. |
| Key Resources | **Key Metrics** | Resources describe what you own. Metrics describe how you'll know the model is working before you own anything. |
| Customer Relationships | **Unfair Advantage** | Relationships (self-service, community, dedicated CSM) are a mature-company choice. What matters earlier is whether the model has any defensibility at all. |

The four common blocks — Customer Segments, Channels, Revenue Streams,
Cost Structure — sit in both. The **Value Proposition** block in the
BMC maps to Maurya's **Unique Value Proposition** with a tighter
early-stage framing (single sentence, aimed at the early adopter, plus
the "high-concept pitch" line).

## Why the swap is right for pre-PMF startups

The Osterwalder BMC was designed to describe *any* business. Its
strength is that a mature company — one with a supply chain, a payroll,
a CRM implementation, and years of customer-relationship history — can
map itself onto the canvas and see the whole thing at once. That
description is a useful diagnostic tool: it shows redundancy,
dependency, and levers you can pull.

A pre-PMF startup has none of those levers. Key Partners doesn't exist;
neither does Key Activities in a stable sense; Key Resources is "our
laptops and each other"; Customer Relationships is "we haven't sold to
anyone yet." Filling those four boxes forces the founder into a
narrative fiction — imagining a mature company they hope to become —
that hides the actual bets. The Lean Canvas's four replacement blocks
force the founder into the questions that actually decide whether the
company survives the next six months: is there a problem, what's the
minimum viable answer, how would we know it's working, and can we
defend it once it does.

Maurya's own framing: **the BMC is optimised for describing what a
business is; the Lean Canvas is optimised for choosing what a business
should bet on.** At IDEA→PRE-SEED, choosing is the whole game.

## When the Business Model Canvas is the right tool

The BMC earns its keep once your business *has* the things the BMC asks
about. Four situations where the BMC is the better choice than the Lean
Canvas:

- **Established / post-PMF company mapping itself.** A Series B+ company
  with real partners, defined activities, real customer-relationship
  choices, and legacy resources to inventory. The BMC's descriptive
  strength is exactly what's useful.
- **Corporate innovation team designing a spinoff or new business unit
  inside a larger company.** Key Partners and Key Resources are non-trivial
  because the corporate parent supplies both — an early-stage lean-canvas
  framing would leave the most consequential blocks empty.
- **Businesses whose defensibility is a network of partnerships.**
  Marketplaces, platforms, and channel-heavy distribution plays — the
  BMC's Key Partners block is where the strategy actually lives, and
  omitting it (as the Lean Canvas does) removes the load-bearing block.
- **JV / M&A strategy design.** Two organisations combining; you need
  to describe both mature businesses' key partners, activities, and
  resources side-by-side before you can see the integration seams. The
  Lean Canvas has no place for that structure.

If you find yourself resenting the Lean Canvas because the empty spot
where Key Partners used to be feels load-bearing for your business,
that resentment is diagnostic: your business is one of the ones the BMC
was designed for.

## When the Lean Canvas is the right tool

Four situations where the Lean Canvas is the better choice:

- **Any pre-PMF startup.** The default. If you don't yet know whether
  the problem is real, what the solution should look like, or what
  metrics matter, you want the four Lean blocks.
- **Any new product line inside an existing company, run as a startup.**
  The internal-startup team has resources but the *product* is pre-PMF;
  the Lean Canvas is what forces them out of "we already have
  distribution" thinking.
- **When you're stuck.** If you've filled a BMC three times and the
  business keeps not working, the Problem / Solution / Key Metrics /
  Unfair Advantage blocks will usually surface what the BMC was letting
  you paper over.
- **When you're pivoting.** A pivot is by definition a return to
  pre-PMF; the Lean Canvas is the right instrument for the redo.

## A defensible answer to "which should we use?"

The question a co-founder or investor might ask — *"why not the
Business Model Canvas?"* — has a two-sentence defensible answer:

> *We're pre-PMF. The four blocks the Lean Canvas replaces (Key
> Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, Customer Relationships)
> would be aspirational for us — the load-bearing bets right now are
> the four blocks Maurya swapped in (Problem, Solution, Key Metrics,
> Unfair Advantage), so those are the ones we want on the page.*

The inverse defense — *"we chose the BMC because we're post-PMF and
partners / activities are the actual strategic choices"* — is equally
legitimate for the businesses it fits. The right posture is neither
tool tribalism nor "use both": pick the one that puts your **load-bearing
bets** on the page, and be able to say which bets those are.

## A shared discipline: one page

Both canvases share the "one page" rule. If yours doesn't fit, the
diagnosis is the same regardless of which template: you're describing
optionality instead of committing to a bet. Cut until it fits.

## Summary

- **BMC and Lean Canvas share** four right-hand blocks (Segments,
  Channels, UVP/Value Proposition, Revenue) and Cost Structure.
- **The Lean Canvas swaps** BMC's Key Partners / Key Activities / Key
  Resources / Customer Relationships for **Problem / Solution / Key
  Metrics / Unfair Advantage**, because those are the actually
  load-bearing bets before PMF.
- Use the **Lean Canvas** at IDEA→PRE-SEED, for pivots, and for
  internal-startup product lines.
- Use the **BMC** for mature businesses, corporate innovation with
  meaningful partner/resource dependencies, partnership-defined
  businesses, and JV / M&A design.
- The choice is not tribal — it's about **which four blocks contain
  your load-bearing bets**.
