# Exercise 02 — Riskiest Assumption Experiment Design

**Module:** 002 Lean Business Modeling · **Stage:** IDEA→PRE-SEED · **Time:**
~2 focused hours · **Prereq exercise:**
[exercise 01](./exercise-01-canvas-and-unit-economics.md) — you need the
filled canvas and unit-economics sheet before you can name the riskiest
assumption defensibly. · **Reads with:** chapter
[05](../05-riskiest-assumption-experiment.md).

## Problem statement

Exercise 01 produced a canvas and a unit-economics sheet with a diagnosis
line naming the single input that, if halved, would break the model.
That diagnosis is the raw material for the most important founder
discipline at IDEA→PRE-SEED: **naming the load-bearing bet and designing
the cheapest experiment that would tell you whether it holds.**

This exercise makes that discipline concrete. You will pick the single
riskiest assumption in your model, write it in the four-part form from
[chapter 05](../05-riskiest-assumption-experiment.md), design the
cheapest experiment that would meaningfully change your belief about it,
and pre-declare the pass/fail condition. Two focused hours here saves
weeks of building the wrong thing.

## Deliverable (one artifact)

A **riskiest-assumption experiment brief** — one page — with:

- The **single riskiest assumption**, in the one-sentence form from
  chapter 05 ("*The business fails if [assumption] is wrong, because
  [downstream consequence]. We would notice it's wrong if [observable
  signal].*").
- A **two-property justification** — consequence and uncertainty —
  showing why *this* assumption beats the two or three runners-up in
  the high-consequence, high-uncertainty cell.
- The **cheapest experiment** that would meaningfully change your
  belief about it, in one paragraph: what you'll do, how, and to whom.
- A **pre-declared pass/fail condition** — specific enough that you
  couldn't rationalise a failed outcome into a pass.
- A **cost estimate** — dollars, days, calendar weeks.
- A **decision rule** — if the experiment passes, what changes on the
  canvas / sheet? If it fails, what changes? If neither (inconclusive),
  what's the follow-up?

## Scenario

Use the same scenario you chose in
[exercise 01](./exercise-01-canvas-and-unit-economics.md). Your
riskiest-assumption call is only meaningful against a specific canvas
and unit-economics sheet — you can't identify a load-bearing bet in
the abstract.

## Requirements

The brief has to satisfy each of the following:

- **Single assumption.** Not two. Not "three tied for riskiest." One.
  If two feel equally load-bearing, the discipline is to pick the one
  whose failure would kill the business fastest and hardest, and to
  say so.
- **Written in the four-part form** from chapter 05: the assumption,
  the downstream consequence, and the observable signal that would
  tell you it's wrong.
- **Justified on both properties.** Consequence (its failure kills
  the model) and uncertainty (you don't know if it's true). An
  assumption that's load-bearing but well-evidenced isn't the riskiest;
  something else is.
- **At least two runners-up named**, with a one-sentence reason each
  for why the winner beats them. Naming the runners-up is what forces
  the discipline of *choosing* rather than *listing*.
- **Experiment matches the assumption class.** Willingness-to-pay
  gets a landing-page smoke test / LOI / concierge MVP at price. A
  channel gets a 50-target outbound test / paid-ad test / community
  post. Retention gets a design-partner cohort with weekly usage
  capture. Cost-to-serve gets a real-usage-load pilot on production
  infra. Chapter 05's menu is the reference.
- **Experiment is genuinely cheap.** Days-not-months, dollars-not-tens-
  of-thousands, and does not require building the product first. If
  your proposed experiment is "we'll build the product and see," you
  have not internalised the cheapest-experiment principle — go back
  and redesign.
- **Pass/fail condition is pre-declared.** "This passes if X happens;
  fails if Y happens." Both X and Y are specific enough that a peer
  reading them cold would agree what counts as which.
- **Decision rule is written.** If the experiment passes, name the
  canvas/sheet change. If it fails, name the pivot / kill / re-test
  path. If neither, name the follow-up experiment.

## Starter guidance

**Step 1 — List every assumption on the canvas and unit-economics
sheet** (~20 min). Every block; every input line. Ten to twenty items
is normal. This is the raw pool the riskiest bet is drawn from.

**Step 2 — Rate each on consequence and uncertainty** (~20 min).
Two-by-two: high/low consequence × high/low uncertainty. Circle the
high/high cell. Usually two or three assumptions land there.

**Step 3 — Pick the single winner** (~10 min). Among the high/high
candidates, pick the one whose failure would kill the business fastest
and hardest. Write it in the four-part form from chapter 05. Name the
two runners-up and why they lost.

**Step 4 — Match the assumption to an experiment class** (~10 min).
Use chapter 05's menu (willingness-to-pay, working channel, retention,
cost-to-serve). If your assumption doesn't map cleanly, describe why
and adapt an experiment; note the adaptation.

**Step 5 — Design the experiment** (~40 min). Write one paragraph:
what you'll do, how, to whom, over what window, and what you'll
measure. Include a channel plan (how you'll get in front of enough
segment-fit people to get signal), a script or landing-page-copy
outline, and the specific metric you'll capture.

**Step 6 — Pre-declare the pass/fail condition and cost** (~10 min).
Write "this passes if X" and "this fails if Y." Be specific enough
that both are unmistakably distinguishable. Estimate dollars, days,
and calendar weeks. If the cost is more than a week of calendar and
a few hundred dollars, ask whether there is a cheaper experiment
that would tell you *most* of what the expensive one would.

**Step 7 — Write the decision rule** (~10 min). If pass: what changes
on the canvas / sheet? If fail: what's the pivot, kill, or re-test?
If inconclusive: what's the follow-up experiment?

## Acceptance criteria

You are done when *all* of the following are true:

- [ ] The brief names **one** riskiest assumption, written in the
      four-part chapter-05 form.
- [ ] **Two or more runners-up are named**, with a one-sentence reason
      each for why the winner beats them.
- [ ] The **experiment matches the assumption class** (willingness-to-
      pay, channel, retention, or cost-to-serve).
- [ ] The **experiment is cheap** — days of calendar, hundreds (not
      thousands or tens of thousands) of dollars, and does not require
      building the product first.
- [ ] The **pass/fail condition is pre-declared** and specific enough
      that a peer reading it cold would agree what counts as which.
- [ ] A **cost estimate** is given in dollars, days of founder time,
      and calendar weeks.
- [ ] A **decision rule** names what changes on the canvas or sheet
      for pass, fail, and inconclusive outcomes.
- [ ] A skeptical co-founder or peer reading the brief would agree
      that (a) the assumption named is genuinely the riskiest, and
      (b) the experiment, if it failed, would actually change your
      belief.

## Self-assessment rubric

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption | "Getting distribution" / "product-market fit" | One specific input from the canvas or sheet, in the four-part form |
| Consequence-uncertainty match | Well-evidenced or low-consequence | High-consequence and genuinely uncertain (in the high/high cell) |
| Runners-up | Absent — winner named without alternatives | Two named with one-sentence reasons for why they lost |
| Experiment class fit | Generic "we'll validate it" | Specific experiment class matched to assumption class |
| Cost | Weeks of engineering / thousands of dollars | Days of calendar, hundreds of dollars, no product build |
| Pass/fail specificity | "We'll see if it works" | X passes / Y fails / Z inconclusive, each specific |
| Decision rule | Absent or "we'll figure it out" | Pass, fail, and inconclusive outcomes each mapped to a next move |
| Skeptic pass | Co-founder shrugs | Co-founder agrees the assumption is riskiest and the test would flip your call |

## Definition of done

A skeptical co-founder reading your brief would agree that the
assumption you named is the riskiest bet on the canvas, that the
experiment you designed would actually change your belief if it
failed, and that you could run it starting Monday for less than the
cost of a business trip. If they instead ask "why not [some other
assumption]?" and you don't have a defensible one-sentence answer,
your consequence/uncertainty analysis in step 2 wasn't sharp enough
— go back and redo it.
