# Chapter 03 — The Investor Funnel: Segmented Targets, Named Conversion Assumptions

> **Reads with:** module objective 3 — *design an investor funnel
> with segmented targets (stage focus, sector fit, check-size / lead
> behaviour, warm-path score) and named conversion assumptions at
> each stage (target → first meeting ~15–25%, first → partner meeting

## Fundraising is a pipeline problem

The story matters — mod-002 built the wedge, mod-003 built the
model, and [chapter 06](./06-pitch-matched-to-stage.md) will match
the pitch to the stage. But whether a round *closes* is a mechanical
function of the top-of-funnel size and the conversion rate at each
stage below it. Founders who treat fundraising as story-only end up
depending on luck; founders who treat it as pipeline-plus-story
close reliably.

The math is unforgiving. If your funnel converts **20%** target →
first meeting, **25%** first → partner, and **20%** partner → term
sheet, a list of 50 investors produces on average **0.5** term
sheets. That is not a round; that is a coin flip. A list of 200,
run in parallel over 6–8 weeks with warm intros and momentum,
produces two or three term sheets — enough to pick a partner, not
just take the only offer you got.

This chapter is about designing the funnel so that the number that
falls out the bottom is not a surprise.

## The four funnel stages

A round has four stages, and it is worth thinking of them as a
funnel with a **conversion rate at each step**:

1. **Target list.** Named investors you have decided are worth
   pitching. Not "VCs"; a specific fund, at a specific stage, with
   a specific partner who invests in your sector.
2. **First meeting.** A 30–45 minute intro call, usually with a
   single partner or principal. Its only job is to earn a second
   meeting.
3. **Partner meeting.** The full partnership hears the pitch. In
   most funds this is where the investment decision effectively
   gets made — the following diligence work is confirmation, not
   underwriting.
4. **Term sheet.** A written offer with a price, an amount, and
   standard terms. Not money in the bank, but the first commitment
   that costs the fund political capital to walk away from.

Rough conversion heuristics from cold-to-warm outbound with a
competent pitch:

- **Target → first meeting: 15–25%**
- **First meeting → partner meeting: 20–35%**
- **Partner meeting → term sheet: 15–25%**
- **End-to-end (target → term sheet): ≈ 1–3%**

The exact numbers matter less than the arithmetic they force. Work
backwards from your **required** term-sheet count.

## The backwards arithmetic

Every raise needs **≥ 2 credible term sheets** to have a real
choice — otherwise you take the only offer, which turns the
negotiation into a monologue. Some rounds need more (the lead is
particularly picky, the fund politics are complex, the market is
soft); rarely fewer.

Working backwards:

```
required term sheets           : ≥ 2
implied partner meetings       : 2 / 0.20     ≈ 10
implied first meetings         : 10 / 0.275   ≈ 36
implied targets                : 36 / 0.20    ≈ 180
```

That is the arithmetic behind the **100–200 target** rule of thumb
you see repeated in seed-fundraising guides. It is not a marketing
number; it is what the funnel actually requires to produce more
than one term sheet at end-to-end conversion rates in the
1–3% range.

Two implications:

- **A short target list is a decision to depend on luck.** Founders
  reach for the "10 dream investors" list when the round is going
  badly. The list is almost never the right answer — it produces
  0–1 term sheets on average and leaves the founder either
  begging the one bidder or restarting the process from scratch.
- **The list has to be real.** A "target" is a fund plus a partner
  plus a plausible warm path. 200 fund names in a spreadsheet with
  no partner assigned and no intro path is not a target list; it
  is a wishlist that will convert at the *cold* end of the range
  (≤ 5% target → first meeting), not the warm end.

## Segmenting the target list

A 200-investor list is not one list — it is several stacks, and
treating them uniformly is what makes an under-prepared founder
look under-prepared. Segment along at least **four axes**:

### Stage focus

Does this fund **lead pre-seed**, **lead seed**, follow at seed,
follow at Series A, or price only Series A and up? A Series A
partner is not just "an investor" for a $2M round — they're the
wrong customer entirely, and the pitch, the ask, and the round
mechanics don't match.

Sources of truth for stage focus, in decreasing order of
reliability:

- The fund's own stated stage focus on their web site — but check
  the "recent investments" page, not the "about us" page, because
  funds drift.
- Recent portfolio: what stage does the fund actually write checks
  at in the last 12 months? "Seed-stage fund" that only led three
  seeds last year is behaving like something else.
- Public statements by the partner (podcast appearances, blog
  posts) on what they're looking for.

### Sector fit

Have they invested in something **adjacent** in the last 18–24
months? Not "have they ever done a company like this," but "are
they actively deploying in this space right now?"

The signals of a partner who is actively deploying in your sector:

- One or more recent adjacent investments (announced in the last
  12–18 months).
- Written or spoken thesis material that names the sector.
- Portfolio companies whose founders they can put you on the phone
  with.

A partner who "covers the space broadly" but hasn't done a deal in
it in three years is not a sector match. They will hear you
politely and pass; the *reason* will be structural (the fund's
current thesis is elsewhere) but you will not be told that. Save
the warm intro for someone actively deploying.

### Check size and lead behaviour

Does this fund typically write a **$2M check as a lead**, a **$500k
check as a follow**, or something in between? A follower can fill
out a round but cannot start one. A round with no lead candidate
in the target list is a round with no plausible path to close.

For each fund, mark:

- **Typical lead check ($).** Zero if they don't lead.
- **Typical follow check ($).** Zero if they don't follow.
- **Recent deals where they were the named lead.** Public announcement
  language ("led by X, with participation from Y and Z") is a
  reliable tell.

A healthy seed target list has **10–30 credible lead candidates**
(funds that have led rounds of your size in the last 12 months,
with a plausible fit for your sector) and a much longer tail of
followers. If your credible-lead count is under 10, the raise
depends on converting a very high fraction of a very small pool —
usually too high a fraction to underwrite.

### Warm-path score

Rank each target by how strong an intro you can get. A rough
anchor scale:

- **9 — founder-they-backed who exited well.** Highest-signal warm
  path in the ecosystem. Usually results in a same-week first
  meeting.
- **7 — current portfolio-company founder or an operator they
  respect** who has met you and would introduce with a real
  endorsement, not a forward.
- **5 — mutual acquaintance who has worked with them and would
  send a considered intro** (not a forward, but not a rave).
- **3 — mutual acquaintance who "could probably intro"** without
  strong prior context. Better than cold, worse than a real
  endorsement.
- **1 — cold LinkedIn, cold email, cold conference intro.** Not
  zero — cold outbound converts sometimes — but the base rate is
  low enough that a target-list dominated by 1s will not produce a
  round.

The quality of the intro moves target → first-meeting conversion by
**3–5×**. A warm-path-9 intro to an active-thesis partner might
convert at **70–80%**; a cold approach to the same partner might
convert at **3–5%**. Segmenting by warm-path score is not a
bookkeeping nicety — it is what makes tier-A rates realistic and
tier-C rates honest.

## Turning segmentation into tiers

The four axes collapse into a **three-tier bucketing** most founders
find workable:

- **Tier A — perfect fit + strong warm path (score ≥ 6).** Active
  in the sector, writes checks of the right size, plausible lead
  candidate, warm intro at hand. This is the small, precious tier —
  usually **15–25 funds** out of a 200-target list. Pitch these
  with a version of the deck you have already stress-tested on a
  practice batch (see
  [chapter 04](./04-parallel-time-boxed-process.md)).
- **Tier B — good fit, decent path (score 3–5).** Right stage,
  sector-adjacent, either a plausible lead or a strong follow, warm
  path via a mutual acquaintance. Usually **50–80 funds** out of
  200. Open in the same window as tier A, on lower expected
  conversion.
- **Tier C — long-shot fit or cold path.** Peripheral sector fit,
  a lead who might follow, or a good fit reached only via cold
  outbound. The rest — **100–130 funds**. Convert at the low end of
  every rate; useful for filling followers into an already-warm
  round.

If your **tier-A count is under 10**, the funnel is fragile — the
raise depends on converting a high fraction of a very small pool.
The first move is not to open the round; the first move is to spend
a week on **warm-path building** (portfolio-founder intros, thesis
partners you can reach through your investors-to-be, conference
one-on-ones) until the tier-A count is defensibly north of 15.

## Applying tier-specific conversion rates

Do not apply one flat conversion to all 200 targets. Warm-path
score dominates the **target → first meeting** step, so:

- **Tier A (warm path ≥ 6):** target → first meeting **50–70%**.
- **Tier B (warm path 3–5):** target → first meeting **15–25%**.
- **Tier C (warm path 1–2 or cold):** target → first meeting
  **1–5%**.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for tier-specific target → first-meeting conversion rates; the split by warm-path score is a common heuristic among fundraising coaches but exact origin varies. -->

The **first → partner** and **partner → term sheet** stages are
less tier-dependent, once you're in the room. Apply the module
heuristics (**20–35%** and **15–25%**) unless you have your own
defensible numbers.

The full arithmetic then looks like:

```
Tier A: 20 targets × 60% × 30% × 20% = 0.72 term sheets
Tier B: 65 targets × 20% × 27% × 20% = 0.70 term sheets
Tier C: 115 targets × 3% × 22% × 18% = 0.14 term sheets
                                       ──────────────
Total implied term sheets              ≈ 1.56
```

That is **less than the ≥ 2 threshold**, which tells you the funnel
as designed will probably not produce a real choice. Fixes, in
order of leverage:

- **Grow tier A** (usually the largest lever). Move five tier-B
  funds into tier A by upgrading the warm path — a real intro from
  a portfolio founder — and the term-sheet count moves visibly.
- **Grow the total list** (moderate lever). Adding 40 tier-B
  funds to a well-targeted list moves the number less than five
  tier-A upgrades.
- **Shrink the round** (last-resort lever). If the funnel simply
  cannot produce 2 sheets at any credible size, the round size
  might be wrong for the current evidence (see
  [chapter 02](./02-raise-amount-milestone-dilution.md)).

## The four common wrong versions

- **"VCs" as a category.** No named funds, no named partners, no
  stage segmentation. Every conversion assumption applied to this
  list is a story the founder is telling themselves.
- **Every warm-path score is an 8.** Founders inflate the warm-path
  score to make the tier-A count look bigger. The funnel then
  predicts term sheets that never arrive. If you can't name the
  specific person doing the intro, the score is a 1, not a 5.
- **Same conversion rate for all 200.** Applying "20% target →
  first meeting" flat, without segmenting on warm-path score,
  makes tier-C look productive when in reality it is close to
  zero.
- **Wishful term-sheet count without a credible lead.** The funnel
  can predict 2 term sheets and still fail to produce a round if
  neither of them comes from a fund that leads. Filter the
  implied-sheet count by lead capability, not just by fund count.

## Summary

- Fundraising is a **pipeline problem**. The number of term sheets
  that fall out is a mechanical function of top-of-funnel size and
  stage-by-stage conversion.
- The funnel has four stages: target → first meeting → partner
  meeting → term sheet, with rough conversions of **15–25%**,
  **20–35%**, and **15–25%** at each step.
- Segment the target list along **four axes** — stage focus, sector
  fit, check-size / lead behaviour, warm-path score — and bucket
  into A / B / C tiers. Apply tier-specific target → first-meeting
  conversion rates.
- A healthy funnel implies **≥ 2 term sheets**, with **≥ 1
  credible lead** among them. If not, grow tier A before opening
  the round.
