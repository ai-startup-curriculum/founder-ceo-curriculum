# Chapter 04 — Running the Round in Parallel: Practice Batch, A-Tier in Parallel, Hard Close Date

> **Reads with:** module objective 4 — *run a round as a parallel,
> time-boxed process — practice batch first, A-tier in parallel with
> a hard close date — rather than sequentially, and understand why
> sequential leaks the round.*

## Momentum is asymmetric

A round has **momentum**, and momentum is asymmetric: you generate
it slowly and lose it fast. Every week the process is open without
a next step is a week your soft signals decay; every week the
process is open with a credible next step is a week competing
partners hear about it and pay attention.

The single decision that most determines whether a founder's
mechanically-fine funnel (see
[chapter 03](./03-investor-funnel-and-conversion.md)) produces two
term sheets or one is the decision to run the round **in parallel,
time-boxed**, rather than sequentially. Sequential fundraising
leaks the round; parallel fundraising concentrates it into a window
that produces choice.

This chapter is about that decision and the three disciplines that
make it work.

## Why sequential leaks the round

The tempting model is one-at-a-time: pitch a partner, wait for the
response, adjust, pitch the next partner, wait, adjust. It feels
tidy, respectful of everyone's time, and low-drama. It is also how
rounds die.

Four things go wrong when you pitch sequentially:

- **The pitch never gets pressure-tested at scale.** The first three
  meetings surface the questions you were unprepared for. By the
  time you know the answers, the first three partners have already
  passed — you can't reach back and re-pitch them, and you've spent
  three of your best warm intros on a version of the deck you now
  wish you hadn't sent.
- **The market talks to itself.** Venture is a small ecosystem. By
  the time investor #5 hears the pitch, investor #4 has told a
  friend at #6 that they passed, and #6 has already softened before
  the first meeting. Sequential process is public information you
  don't get to see.
- **You lose the FOMO lever.** The single most powerful sentence in
  a seed process is some version of *"we have a soft-circled lead
  and are closing on \[date\]."* Sequential process makes that
  sentence impossible; nobody has soft-circled because nobody has
  been asked to move at the same time as anyone else.
- **The round drifts open indefinitely.** Without a batch, there is
  no reason to close. Meetings extend, the process stretches from
  8 weeks to 20, and by month five the founder is quoting a
  seven-figure raise that is really a $500k rolling angel round
  with a lot of "we're still building conviction" replies.

The math from [chapter 03](./03-investor-funnel-and-conversion.md)
is unforgiving: at a 1–3% end-to-end conversion, a sequential
process reaches only a small fraction of the funnel before its
momentum decays. Even if the underlying funnel *could* produce
three term sheets in parallel, run sequentially it will produce
zero or one.

## Discipline 1 — the practice batch

**Pitch a small batch of weaker-fit investors first**, before you
open the round to your A-tier.

The practice batch does three things:

- **Stress-tests the deck.** The questions you can't answer, the
  slides that don't land, the numbers that don't survive the first
  challenge — the practice batch surfaces them at a cost your
  round can afford.
- **Rehearses the founder.** Pitching is a skill; the tenth pitch
  is measurably better than the first. Do the first ten on
  investors whose "no" is not a cost, so the eleventh through
  hundredth are on investors whose "yes" is the round.
- **Calibrates your funnel assumptions.** If the practice batch
  converts at 5% target → first meeting when your funnel model
  assumed 20%, you learn that in a week — not in month three,
  when the A-tier funnel is already producing the same result and
  you've spent your best intros.

Concrete recipe:

- **Pick 10–20 targets** that are B-tier or C-tier — good enough
  that the meeting is real, weak enough that a "no" isn't a
  problem for the round.
- **Pitch them in the same week or two.** Not one at a time — the
  practice batch is itself a small parallel process.
- **Between the practice batch and the A-tier open, revise the
  deck.** Every question the practice batch surfaced that you
  fumbled becomes a slide, an appendix, or a rehearsed answer.

The practice batch is what stops you from burning your best
warm intros on version-one of the deck. It is the single cheapest
insurance policy in the raise; skipping it is the most common
correctable mistake in a first-time founder's process.

## Discipline 2 — A-tier in parallel

Once the deck has survived the practice batch, **open the A-tier
funds in parallel** — in a single week or two, not spread over
months.

What "in parallel" concretely means:

- **First meetings booked in the same 2–3 week window.** Not "we'll
  start reaching out and see who's available"; a target date after
  which the calendar is committed.
- **Warm intros requested in a batch.** A one-week sprint of
  warm-intro requests to the 15–25 A-tier funds, with the same
  short paragraph adapted to each intro-maker. Warm intros
  requested one-at-a-time drift; warm intros requested in a batch
  land on the same news cycle.
- **A common set of materials.** One deck, one data-room link, one
  metrics one-pager — the same across the whole A-tier batch. If
  you're customising the deck per fund you're multiplying the
  process burden without much return, and it makes the pipeline
  harder to track.
- **A pipeline tracker.** A single spreadsheet with fund, partner,
  intro source, first-meeting date, next step, and the date of the
  last touch. This is the operating instrument of the round — not
  optional, and reviewed at least twice a week.

The point is not to be rude to any single partner. The point is to
compress the *market-facing* window into something short enough
that momentum accumulates before it decays. A partner who hears
about you on Monday, sees three friends mention you on Tuesday, and
gets your intro-forward on Wednesday is a partner who has already
started prioritising the meeting. Sequential process denies you
that compounding effect.

## Discipline 3 — the hard close date

**Set a target close date up front and tell investors, when asked,
that you're aiming to close by then.**

A dateless round is a round that never closes. A dated round
creates the FOMO that produces competing term sheets — the
sentence *"we're aiming to close by \[date\] and would need to know
before then"* is what turns a "we're building conviction" reply
into a decision.

Guidelines for setting the date:

- **6–8 weeks from the first A-tier pitch** is a common target for
  a well-prepared seed process. Shorter and the deck usually
  hasn't survived pressure; longer and momentum decays.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the 6–8 week close-window norm; Y Combinator's *A Guide to Seed Fundraising* and Paul Graham's *How to Raise Money* both describe short parallel processes, but exact figures vary. -->
- **The date is a deadline for term sheets, not for wires.**
  Between term sheet and cash in the bank is another **2–6
  weeks** of diligence and legal (see
  [chapter 08](./08-running-the-close.md)); the close window is
  about locking in the *decision*, not the money.
- **Communicate the date consistently.** The date is on the
  tracker, in the founder's calendar, and — most importantly — in
  the reply to every partner who asks about timing. Different
  answers to different partners defeat the purpose.

The date should be plausible. A three-week close on a $5M seed
raise from an unproven founder is unbelievable, and partners will
discount it. A six-week close on the same raise is aggressive but
credible; an eight-week close is comfortable. Pick the number you
can defend if a partner asks *why* that date, and hold it.

## Sequencing the batches

Put the three disciplines together into a calendar:

```
Week -2 / -1  Warm-intro sprint: 15–25 asks for A-tier + 10–20 for practice.
Week  1       Practice batch pitched (10–20 B / C-tier partners).
Week  2       Deck revised from practice-batch feedback.
Week  2–4     A-tier + strong B-tier opened in parallel; first meetings.
Week  3–5     Partner meetings for the funds that advanced.
Week  4–6     Term sheet(s) target; "aiming to close by end of week 8".
Week  6–8     Term sheet negotiation, follower allocation, close window.
Week  8+      Diligence + legal (2–6 weeks), wire.
```

Two things this calendar deliberately implies:

- **The round is a project, not a mode.** There is a start date, a
  target close date, and a "the round is over" transition after
  which the founder goes back to running the company. A rolling
  raise is not a round; it is a founder who has not turned back
  to building.
- **Everything downstream of the practice batch is compressed.**
  Weeks 2 through 6 are the momentum window; if the calendar
  stretches across months in that middle band, momentum is gone
  and the round is now sequential.

## When "in parallel" collides with reality

Real calendars don't cooperate. Partners travel. Intros land at
different speeds. A key A-tier fund's partner is on a two-week
family leave. Two rules of thumb:

- **Late arrivals join the batch, they don't restart it.** A
  partner whose intro lands in week 4 gets a first meeting in week
  4 or 5 and is caught up to the same close deadline. Do not
  restart the timeline for a late arrival — that is how sequential
  reasserts itself.
- **Silence past a week is a soft no** (see
  [chapter 07](./07-signal-vs-theater.md)). Drop the partner from
  the active pipeline and move on; you can always re-open the
  conversation after the round closes with a "we closed on \[date\],
  wanted to keep you posted" note.

A parallel process that occasionally accommodates real schedules is
still parallel. A "parallel" process where every partner sets their
own timeline is really sequential wearing parallel-shaped clothing.

## The four common wrong versions

- **One-at-a-time, waiting for each response.** The most common
  first-time-founder mistake. Feels polite; guarantees the round
  leaks momentum before it produces a lead.
- **All-in on tier A with no practice batch.** The deck is
  version-one, the founder is pitching for the first time, and
  the best warm intros are burned on the version of the process
  that is worst prepared.
- **Rolling raise with no close date.** "We're closing on a rolling
  basis" invites drift, gives followers no reason to move, and
  produces smaller rounds than the founder could have raised.
- **Different close-date answers to different partners.** The
  founder tells one partner "end of April" and another "we're not
  in a hurry" to seem accommodating. Both partners talk; the
  discrepancy reads as unseriousness and kills the FOMO lever.

## Summary

- Rounds have **momentum**, and momentum is asymmetric — generated
  slowly, lost fast. The single most impactful decision in the
  round is whether to run it **in parallel or sequentially**.
- The three disciplines: a **practice batch** to stress-test the
  deck at low cost; **A-tier in parallel** to compress the
  market-facing window; a **hard close date** to convert
  "building conviction" into a decision.
- **6–8 weeks** from first A-tier pitch to target close is a
  common calendar; the date is for term sheets, with another **2–6
  weeks** of diligence and legal before wires.
- A parallel process that accommodates real calendars is still
  parallel; a "parallel" process where every partner sets their
  own timeline is sequential.
