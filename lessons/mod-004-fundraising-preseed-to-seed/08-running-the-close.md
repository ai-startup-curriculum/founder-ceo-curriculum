# Chapter 08 — Running the Close: Keep the Pipeline Warm, Bank the Round, Email the Passers

> **Reads with:** module objective 8 — *run the close: hold the
> pipeline warm until the wire clears, bank the round in a
> scheduled window with a hard close date, and email the passers
> when the round closes.*

## The round isn't over at the term sheet

The most seductive lie in fundraising is that the round is over
when the lead signs the term sheet. It isn't. Between term sheet
and wire is typically **2–6 weeks** of legal, diligence, and
follower allocation — and in that gap, followers disappear,
diligence surfaces surprises, and a founder who has stopped
running the process ends up with a smaller round than they thought
they had.

The close has three disciplines that keep the round from slipping:

- **Keep the pipeline warm until the wire clears.**
- **Bank the round in a scheduled window with a hard close date.**
- **Email the passers when the round closes.**

Each is a small operational move. Each is also the difference
between a $3M round closing at $3M and one closing at $2.1M.

## Discipline 1 — keep the pipeline warm until the wire clears

The temptation is to stop taking meetings the moment the lead signs
the term sheet. Don't. Two failure modes make this the most costly
mistake at the close.

- **Followers disappear during the diligence gap.** A follower who
  said "we're in" the day after the term sheet may quietly go
  silent by day 20. Their fund's investment committee reconsiders,
  a competing deal appears, the partner traveling for two weeks
  loses momentum. If your pipeline is dead, you have no
  replacement — the follower slot goes unfilled and the round
  closes smaller.
- **Diligence surfaces surprises that jeopardise the lead
  itself.** A reference call that goes badly, a legal issue that
  turns up during due diligence, a competitive announcement that
  changes the fund's read — any of these can turn a signed term
  sheet into a "we need to reconsider" conversation. Term sheets
  are typically non-binding; the lead can walk.

The concrete moves:

- **Keep taking first meetings with credible followers** until the
  wire lands. New follower conversations do not require you to be
  "still raising" in the market-facing sense — you can honestly
  describe the round as "priced, closing on \[date\], with room
  for a small number of additional follow-on checks."
- **Communicate the lead early to the strong follower pipeline.**
  "We have a term sheet from \[lead\] at \[price\], closing by
  \[date\], and there's allocation for \[$X\]" turns a slow
  follower conversation into a fast one. Named momentum
  concentrates decisions.
- **Do not spend a dollar of the round before the wire lands.**
  Cash is only cash when it's in the account (mod-003, chapter
  01). Signed term sheet, signed round documents, and even signed
  subscription agreements are not cash. The founder who commits
  to a hire against a signed-but-unwired round has learned this
  lesson the expensive way at least once.
- **Track diligence items actively.** A shared checklist of
  outstanding diligence requests — customer references, legal
  docs, financials — makes the founder responsible for
  unblocking the close instead of waiting on the lead. Most
  diligence delays are founder-side; treating them as
  founder-owned is what compresses the gap.

## Discipline 2 — bank the round in a scheduled window

A four-week window with a **hard close date** focuses everyone.
An open-ended "we're closing on a rolling basis" invites drift,
gives followers no reason to move, and produces smaller rounds
than the founder could have raised.

The close window has three anchors:

- **The lead's term sheet target close date.** Usually 3–4 weeks
  from the term-sheet signing, allowing for legal drafting and
  the lead's diligence to finalise.
- **The follower allocation deadline.** Typically 1–2 weeks
  earlier than the lead's close, so that follower checks are in
  the round docs before the lead signs. Communicated as *"if you
  want allocation, we need confirmation by \[date\]."*
- **The wire target date.** Typically 3–7 days after signature,
  depending on jurisdiction, bank speeds, and whether the lead's
  fund is doing a capital call to fund the wire.

The founder's job in the close window is to keep those three dates
visible to everyone involved and to push relentlessly on the
milestones each requires.

The other move: **do not let the round drift past the close date
without a specific reason**. If the close slips a week because the
lead's lawyer had a family emergency, fine — reset the target
publicly with the new date. If the close slips a week because
"things are just busy," that is the founder losing the round. The
close-date discipline exists because someone has to force the
decisions; if the founder abdicates that role, no one else
picks it up.

### The "open the round, close the round" transition

At some point the round is over — the wire lands, the docs are
countersigned, the follower allocation is locked. The founder's
next job is to **explicitly close the round**:

- **Announce internally** — to the team, the co-founders, and any
  advisors — that the round is closed.
- **Stop taking investor meetings.** The next round is not the
  same conversation as this one; scheduling investor meetings
  the week after a close is a signal to the market that the
  round didn't really close.
- **Return to running the company.** Fundraising is a mode the
  founder is in; the round is over when the mode ends. Founders
  who don't consciously make the transition end up in a low-grade
  perpetual raise that never becomes anything.

## Discipline 3 — email the passers

Once the round is closed, **email every investor who passed**.

This is one of the highest-leverage small moves in fundraising.
Investors remember which founders keep them in the loop; passers
who see you close a round they turned down update their model of
you, and the founders who write the closing note are on the
shortlist for the *next* round's first meetings.

The note is short. A template that works:

```
Subject: We closed our seed

Hi [Partner],

Thanks again for the time on [date]. Wanted to close the loop:
we closed our seed today, led by [Lead], with participation from
[Followers]. Total raise: $[X] at $[Y] post-money.

Since we last talked, the specific things that moved:
  - [one concrete outcome or metric]
  - [one concrete outcome or metric]
  - [one concrete outcome or metric]

The next 18 months are about [milestone]. I'll keep you posted
quarterly, and would love to talk again as we approach the next
round.

Best,
[Founder]
```

Three notes on the note:

- **It is honest.** No inflated language, no defensive framing of
  their pass. "We closed" is the fact; nothing else needs to be
  argued.
- **It leads with what changed.** The specific outcomes since the
  meeting show that the fund's read at the time is now
  out-of-date. This is the fact that will change their next
  read.
- **It sets the cadence.** "Quarterly updates" tells the passer
  what to expect and what not to expect. It also implicitly
  invites them onto the next round's warm list, without any
  pressure.

Founders who send this note to 40–60 passers after a seed close
routinely find that 5–15% of those same funds are the ones who
start real conversations at the Series A — the pass was on
"not-enough-evidence-yet," and the closing note is what keeps the
door open for when the evidence lands.

## The two special-case closes

Two situations deserve explicit callouts because they trip up
first-time founders even when the standard close discipline is in
place.

### The soft-circled lead that goes silent

Occasionally a lead soft-circles the round — verbal commitment,
positive partner meeting, next-step named — and then goes silent
for two weeks. Do not wait it out.

- **Send one direct, unambiguous email at day 5.** *"Wanted to
  confirm we're aligned on \[terms\] and can plan to close by
  \[date\]. Do you have a term sheet timeline?"* If the answer is
  vague, the soft-circle is fading.
- **Do not stop the parallel process.** A soft-circled lead is not
  a signed term sheet; if the soft-circle disappears you need
  another live conversation to fall back on. Keep pitching until
  the paper is signed.

### The over-subscribed round

The opposite problem: more allocation demand than the round has
room for. Two mistakes to avoid.

- **Do not casually up-size the round.** Increasing the round
  post-lead-signature changes the dilution math, may require
  re-approval from the lead, and can look mercenary. If you need
  to up-size, do it *once*, deliberately, with the lead's
  agreement, and only if the additional dollars buy more milestone
  — not just more runway.
- **Allocate followers with intent.** An over-subscribed round is
  a chance to concentrate the cap table around the highest-signal
  investors — the operator-angels you actually want on the ask
  list, the follower funds whose partners will be materially
  helpful. It is also a chance to *say no gracefully* to
  low-fit investors, without ever making it about them.

## The four common wrong versions

- **Stopping the process at the term sheet.** Follower allocation
  drops, the diligence gap becomes a hole, and the round closes
  smaller than the term sheet implied.
- **Rolling close with no date.** Followers have no reason to
  move quickly, the diligence gap stretches indefinitely, and the
  round drifts into the founder's next quarter.
- **Spending against unwired money.** A hire is committed, a
  vendor contract is signed, a lease is guaranteed — all before
  the wire lands. When the wire is 30 days late, or the round
  is renegotiated in diligence, the founder is caught by a
  paperwork delay.
- **Not writing to the passers.** The single highest-leverage
  post-close move gets skipped because it feels awkward. The
  passers hear about the close through the grapevine or
  TechCrunch and update their model of the founder without any
  input from the founder.

## Summary

- The round is not over at the term sheet. Between term sheet and
  wire is **2–6 weeks** of legal, diligence, and follower
  allocation.
- **Keep the pipeline warm until the wire clears.** Followers
  disappear during the diligence gap; diligence surprises can
  reopen the lead conversation. Cash is only cash when it's in
  the account.
- **Bank the round in a scheduled window with a hard close date.**
  Communicate the follower deadline, the lead's close target, and
  the wire target; push on each one relentlessly.
- **Email every passer when the round closes.** Short, honest, and
  led with what changed. Passers who see you close a round they
  turned down are the shortlist for the *next* round's first
  meetings.
