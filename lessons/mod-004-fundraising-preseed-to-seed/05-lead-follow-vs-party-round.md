## Rounds have a shape

Two rounds that raise the same total dollars can have very different
*shapes* — and the shape has consequences for which conversations
matter, how the process runs, and how the cap table looks the
morning after close.

The two shapes founders need to recognise:

- **Lead-follow rounds.** A single **lead investor** commits first,
  sets the price and the terms, does the legal work on the round
  documents, and typically takes a board seat. Once the lead is in,
  **followers** stack allocation into the same round on the same
  terms without renegotiating.
- **Party rounds.** No formal lead. A stack of smaller checks — from
  angels, micro-funds, and syndicates — accumulates over weeks or
  months, usually on **SAFEs** with a shared valuation cap. The
  round closes when the founder decides it has closed.

Both are legitimate structures at different stages, and confusing
them is how founders end up running the wrong process for the round
they are actually raising.

## The lead-follow structure

At **seed and up**, the standard shape is a **priced preferred-stock
round with a lead**. Two mechanical facts about the lead:

- **The lead prices the round.** They agree with the founder on the
  pre-money valuation, the total raise size, the option-pool
  refresh, and the specific preferred-share terms (the mechanics
  live in mod-005 and *Venture Deals*). Followers then sign the
  same paperwork at the same price.
- **The lead does the legal work.** The lead's counsel drafts the
  round documents — the series preferred certificate of
  incorporation, the investors' rights agreement, the voting
  agreement, the right-of-first-refusal / co-sale agreement.
  Followers review and sign; they don't redraft.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for standard seed-round paperwork; the National Venture Capital Association (NVCA) publishes standard model financing documents (nvca.org/model-legal-documents) that are widely used as the reference — verify the current URL and version. -->

The lead usually takes a **board seat**, typically 5–20% of the
round for a boutique seed fund and higher for a multi-stage fund
writing a full seed. They also typically get pro-rata rights on the
next round.

Two implications founders often miss:

### The lead is the single hardest yes to get

Everything else in the round follows from the lead's decision. Most
of your calendar effort should be aimed at converting a **small
number of realistic lead candidates**, not spreading effort evenly
across the whole list.

Practically:

- **Your A-tier pipeline is really your lead pipeline.** The 15–25
  A-tier funds from [chapter 03](./03-investor-funnel-and-conversion.md)
  should include at least **10 credible lead candidates** — funds
  that have led rounds of your size in the last 12 months. If your
  lead-capable count is under 10, the round has a structural
  problem no amount of follower momentum will solve.
- **A "verbal yes" from a follower is not a commitment.** Followers
  routinely say yes contingent on a lead pricing the round. That
  contingency is fine — that's the follower's job — but it means
  the round is not "half raised" just because you have $2M of
  follower interest against a $3M raise. Until the lead signs,
  nothing is committed.
- **The lead's diligence sets the pace of the whole round.** Once
  the lead is close, followers move fast; while the lead is still
  building conviction, follower conversations are slow and often
  drift. Focus the tightest, fastest work on the lead candidates
  and let the follower conversations stay open in the background.

### Followers stack allocation on the same terms

The value of the lead-follow structure is **standardisation**.
Once the lead has priced the round, followers do not renegotiate
price, they do not add clauses, they do not draft their own
side-agreements. They tell the lead's counsel how much of the
allocation they want; the lead's counsel adds them to the round;
they sign.

This is what makes the "we have a soft-circled lead, can you
confirm your allocation by \[date\]" call short. Without a lead,
that call is a multi-week negotiation with each follower over
their own terms; with a lead, it is a signature.

If a follower tries to renegotiate the price or add a clause after
the lead has signed, the answer is **no**. Rounds fall apart when
founders make asymmetric concessions to individual followers; the
lead-follow structure exists precisely so those side conversations
don't happen.

### Cap table implications

A well-run lead-follow round produces a **clean cap table**:

- The lead owns 5–20% of the post-money.
- 2–4 named follower funds each own 1–5%.
- A short tail of strategic angels each own 0.25–1%.
- The founders, employees, and any existing angels are the rest.

Every named investor on the cap table has a specific relationship —
you know why they are on it, and you know what to expect from them.
A future investor doing diligence on the cap table sees a coherent
story: a lead that priced the round, a small set of followers who
followed, a small set of angels with clear relevance.

## Party rounds

At **pre-seed**, especially before there is a lead-capable investor
willing to price the round, the common shape is a **party round**
— a stack of smaller checks on SAFEs with no formal lead.

Mechanics:

- **Everyone signs a SAFE** (the Y Combinator standard SAFE, or a
  post-money variant; see mod-005 for the arithmetic) at a
  **shared valuation cap** the founder sets.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the Y Combinator SAFE and post-money SAFE documents; canonical location is ycombinator.com/documents. -->
- **Individual check sizes** range from **$25k to $250k**, mostly
  from angels and micro-funds.
- **No board seat, no protective provisions**, no formal reporting
  covenant beyond what the founder chooses to send.
- **No formal close date.** The round accumulates over weeks or
  months; the founder decides when to stop taking checks.

The lower-friction close is the point. A pre-seed founder can, in
a good week, sign three $50k SAFEs on three different Zoom calls
without touching a lawyer. A lead-follow priced round at the same
stage would take another 6–8 weeks and $30k–$50k of legal fees for
no meaningful pricing benefit.

### What a party round trades away

The lower friction comes with three costs the founder should
recognise:

- **A diffuse cap table.** Fifteen $50k angels on fifteen SAFEs
  are fifteen people to update, fifteen consent signatures on
  future documents, and fifteen relationships to maintain.
  Post-money SAFEs mitigate some of the mechanical dilution
  confusion but do not fix the operational load.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the post-money SAFE and its dilution mechanics; ycombinator.com/documents and the Y Combinator SAFE User Guide are the canonical references. -->
- **A smaller total raise.** Party rounds typically max out
  somewhere in the low millions — beyond that, the founder needs a
  fund willing to write a large check, which is a lead by another
  name.
- **No governance partner.** A lead is a partner you can call at
  10pm about a hiring decision, a hard customer question, a market
  pivot. Fifteen angels are a broadcast list. Both are useful;
  they are not substitutes.

### When a party round is the right choice

Party rounds are the right structure when:

- **You are raising a pre-seed of $500k–$2M** and no lead-capable
  fund is priced-round-ready to commit at your current evidence.
- **The check sizes you can attract** are angel-sized and there is
  no natural anchor to pull a lead in.
- **You want to close quickly** — days or a couple of weeks — to
  extend runway while you build the story that raises the seed.

Party rounds are the *wrong* structure when:

- **You are raising a seed of $2M+**, which almost always needs a
  priced lead. If you cannot find a lead for a seed round, that is
  a signal about the round's readiness, not a structural quirk to
  work around.
- **You want to close a large enough check from a single fund**
  that the fund will demand a priced round anyway (typically once
  a single check crosses roughly $500k–$1M — many funds simply
  will not write that size on a SAFE).

## The hybrid — seed rounds with SAFEs stacked around a priced lead

The two structures are not mutually exclusive. A very common seed
shape looks like this:

- **A priced lead** takes 60–80% of the round on preferred stock.
- **A small set of angels and micro-funds** add another 10–20% on
  SAFEs that convert into the same preferred series at the same
  price.

This gives the founder the discipline of a lead-priced round and
the low-friction addition of angel money on the margin. The lead
still runs the paperwork; the SAFE-holders convert into the round
at conversion. Mod-005 covers the arithmetic; here it is enough to
know the structure exists and is the norm at seed.

## What the founder actually owes each investor type

Different structures create different ongoing relationships:

| Investor type | Structure | Ongoing obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Angel on a SAFE (party round) | No board seat, no formal reporting | Optional quarterly update; keep them on the ask list for follow-on |
| SAFE participant in a lead-priced round | Same as above, converts at next priced round | Same as above; they signed at the lead's price |
| Named follower fund in a priced round | 1–5% of the round | Quarterly update, occasional intro asks, pro-rata expectation |
| Lead investor in a priced round | 5–20% of the round; board seat | Monthly reporting, board meetings, deep strategic partnership |

## The four common wrong versions

- **Treating the whole target list uniformly, with no lead
  candidates identified.** The round has no path to price because
  no one at the table can price it. This is the seed-round version
  of "we have a lot of interest" that never becomes a term sheet.
- **Announcing a "party round" for a seed-size raise.** A $3M
  round of stacked SAFEs from 30 angels is a construction project,
  not a round — the operational load and the cap-table diffusion
  make it strictly worse than finding a lead.
- **Renegotiating with followers after the lead has signed.**
  Rounds fall apart when the founder starts trading terms
  individually with each follower. The whole point of the
  lead-follow structure is that the lead's paper is the paper.
- **Confusing follower "yes-if-priced" for a commitment.** A
  founder who counts follower conditional-yeses toward the raise
  total ends up with 60% of a $3M raise soft-circled and no lead
  — a state that reads to the market as "unfinished," not "almost
  closed."

## Summary

- Rounds have a **shape**: a **lead-follow priced round** (standard
  at seed and up) or a **party round of stacked SAFEs** (common at
  pre-seed).
- In a lead-follow round the **lead is the single hardest yes** —
  they price the round, do the legal, and often take the board
  seat; **followers stack allocation on the same terms** without
  renegotiating.
- A **party round** trades lower-friction close for a diffuse cap
  table, a smaller total raise, and no governance partner. It is
  the right structure at pre-seed and the wrong one at a proper
  seed.
- The hybrid — a **priced lead plus SAFEs stacked around it** — is
  the common shape of a modern seed. Mod-005 covers the arithmetic.
- Board management, composition, and fiduciary duty are out of
  scope for this module. See
  [chapter 09](./09-boundaries-finance-and-governance.md).
