# Module 004 — Fundraising: Pre-seed → Seed

> **Stage:** PRE-SEED→SEED · **Pillar:** Fundraising · **Prereq:** mod-003

## Why this module

Fundraising feels like a story problem, and founders who treat it that way end
up depending on luck. The founders who close reliably treat it as a **pipeline
problem** — one where the story matters, but where the number of term sheets
that fall out the bottom is a mechanical function of how many targets went in
the top and how well each stage converted.

The math is unforgiving. If your funnel converts 20% target → first meeting,
25% first meeting → partner meeting, and 20% partner meeting → term sheet, a
list of 50 investors returns, on average, **half** a term sheet. That is not a
round; that is a coin flip. A list of 200, run in parallel over 6–8 weeks with
warm intros and momentum, returns two or three term sheets — enough to pick a
partner, not just take the only offer you got.

The other failure mode is raising against a vibe instead of a model. Mod-003
built the operating plan; this module raises against it. The right size of the
round, the right stage to raise at, and the right thing to promise investors
all fall out of the zero-cash date and the milestone the round is supposed to
buy. A raise that isn't tied to a milestone is a raise that never ends.

## Learning objectives

After this module you can:

1. Describe the **fundraising ladder** — angels → pre-seed → seed → Series A —
   including typical check sizes, round sizes, and what each stage expects
   from a founder in exchange.
2. Compute **how much to raise** from your mod-003 operating model, tied to a
   specific 12–24 month milestone, with the resulting dilution range stated.
3. Design an **investor funnel** with segmented targets, named conversion
   assumptions at each stage, and the implied number of term sheets.
4. Run a round as a **parallel, time-boxed process** — batched intros,
   momentum, a lead-follow structure — rather than a sequential one that leaks
   optionality with every "no."
5. Distinguish **signal from theater** in investor meetings, and know the
   difference between a soft "keep us posted" and an actual next step.

## Core concepts

### 1. The fundraising ladder

Each stage of financing is a distinct market, with different investors,
different check sizes, and different expectations of the founder. Confusing
them is a common early error — pitching a pre-seed to a Series A partner
wastes both parties' time and burns a warm intro.

- **Friends, family, and angels.** Individual checks of roughly **$10k–$100k**
  from people who invest their own money — sometimes on nothing more than
  belief in the founder. Fast to close, no board seat, no diligence process to
  speak of. Best used to bridge from idea to a fundable pre-seed, or to fill
  out a lead-led round.
- **Pre-seed.** A round of roughly **$500k–$3M** from institutional pre-seed
  funds (Hustle Fund, Precursor, K9, and a growing category of others), angels
  syndicating on AngelList, and occasionally a seed fund taking an early
  position. What they buy is the founder and a plausible wedge; there is often
  no product and no revenue.
- **Seed.** A round of roughly **$2M–$5M** at the traditional end, and
  **$5M–$15M** at the larger "seed extension" / "big seed" end that has become
  common. Seed funds (First Round, Uncork, Founder Collective, etc.) and
  multi-stage funds writing early checks. Investors expect some evidence — an
  MVP, early users, pilots, a signed design partner — enough that the bet is
  no longer purely on the story.
- **Series A.** A round of roughly **$8M–$25M** from a lead partner at a
  multi-stage fund (Sequoia, Benchmark, a16z, Accel, etc.). Series A investors
  price product-market fit; the founder is expected to show retention,
  repeatable acquisition, and a credible path to a Series B milestone.

Each round is expected to buy roughly **12–24 months of runway** and produce
enough evidence to justify the next round at a higher valuation. If a round
doesn't do both, the next round is a bridge or a down-round.

### 2. What a raise is *for* (from mod-003's model)

A raise is not a trophy. It is a purchase of time to hit a specific,
pre-named milestone. Two rules of thumb, both grounded in the operating model
from mod-003:

- **Raise for 18–24 months of runway post-close.** Less than 12 and you're
  raising again the moment the round closes. More than 30 and the dilution is
  usually not worth it — you'd have been better off raising less at the same
  price and topping up later at a higher one.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the 18–24 month post-raise runway norm; commonly repeated in the Y Combinator *Guide to Seed Fundraising* (Geoff Ralston) and Sequoia writings, exact origin varies. -->
- **Raise against a milestone, not a vibe.** The round should buy something an
  A-round investor would recognize as evidence: *"reach $2M ARR with < 5%
  monthly churn,"* *"sign 10 enterprise design partners,"* *"ship v1 and
  demonstrate 30% week-1 retention."* The milestone becomes the story that
  raises the *next* round.

Dilution is the price of the time. At seed, expect to sell **15–25%** of the
company in a priced round (mod-005 walks through the arithmetic). If you're
selling more than 25% at seed, the valuation is too low or the round is too
big; if less than 15%, you likely didn't raise enough or the price was too
high to be defensible later.

### 3. Fundraising as a pipeline

A round has four stages, and it is worth thinking of them as a **funnel** with
a conversion rate at each step:

1. **Target list.** Named investors you have decided are worth pitching. Not
   "VCs"; a specific fund, at a specific stage, with a specific partner who
   invests in your sector.
2. **First meeting.** A 30–45 minute intro call, usually with a single
   partner or principal. Its only job is to earn a second meeting.
3. **Partner meeting.** The full partnership hears the pitch. In most funds
   this is where the investment decision effectively gets made.
4. **Term sheet.** A written offer with a price, an amount, and standard
   terms. Not money, but the first commitment that costs the fund to walk
   away from.

Rough conversion heuristics from cold-to-warm outbound with a competent
pitch:

- Target → first meeting: **15–25%**
- First meeting → partner meeting: **20–35%**
- Partner meeting → term sheet: **15–25%**
- End-to-end (target → term sheet): **~1–3%**
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for stage-by-stage conversion rates in seed fundraising; the numbers here are commonly cited by fundraising coaches (e.g., Elizabeth Yin, Jason Yeh) and vary substantially by warmth of intro, sector, and market cycle. -->

The exact numbers matter less than the arithmetic they force: to reliably
produce **2–3 term sheets** — the minimum for a real choice — you typically
need a target list of **100–200 investors**, run in parallel. A short list is
what founders reach for when the round is going badly; it is almost never
the right answer.

### 4. Segmenting the target list

A 200-investor list is not one list — it is several stacks, and treating them
uniformly is what makes an under-prepared founder look under-prepared. Segment
along at least four axes:

- **Stage focus.** Do they lead pre-seed? Follow at seed? Only price Series A
  and up? A Series A partner is not just "an investor" for a $2M round —
  they're the wrong customer entirely.
- **Sector fit.** Have they invested in something adjacent in the last 24
  months? Their public portfolio, their blog, their podcast appearances are
  all evidence. Investors who are actively deploying in your sector will hear
  you; investors who "cover it broadly" often won't.
- **Check size and lead behavior.** Does this fund typically write a $2M
  check as a lead, a $500k follow, or something in between? A follower can
  fill out a round but cannot start one.
- **Warm-path score.** Rank each target by how strong an intro you can get.
  A founder they backed who exited well is a **9**; a mutual acquaintance
  who "could probably intro" is a **3**; cold LinkedIn is a **1**. The
  quality of the intro moves your first-meeting conversion by 3–5×.

The output of segmenting is a **prioritized tier system**: tier-A (perfect
fit, strong warm path), tier-B (good fit, decent path), tier-C (long-shot fit
or cold path). Which tier you pitch first is a strategic choice — see
momentum, next.

### 5. Momentum, parallelism, and time-boxing the round

Rounds have momentum, and momentum is asymmetric — you generate it slowly and
lose it fast. Three disciplines keep the round moving forward:

- **Run in parallel, not sequentially.** Pitch a batch of 10–20 investors in
  the same week, not one at a time waiting for each to respond. Sequential
  fundraising leaks the round: by the time investor #5 hears the pitch,
  investors #1–4 have either said no (public info in the venture ecosystem)
  or gone silent, and every subsequent conversation starts with an implicit
  "why hasn't anyone else committed?"
- **Time-box the round.** Set a target close date up front — six to eight
  weeks from the first pitch — and tell investors, when asked, that you're
  aiming to close by then. A dateless round is a round that never closes; a
  dated round creates the FOMO that produces competing term sheets.
- **Batch the tiers.** A common structure is to pitch a small **practice
  batch** first (weaker-fit investors whose "no" costs you nothing), fix the
  pitch based on the questions you got, then open the **A-tier batch** in
  parallel with a clear deadline. The practice batch is what stops you from
  burning your best warm intros on version-one of the deck.

Momentum is also what enables the "we have a soft-circled lead, are you in?"
call that closes followers in days rather than weeks. Without a parallel
process, there is no soft-circled lead; without a soft-circled lead, followers
have no reason to move quickly.

### 6. The lead–follow structure of a round

Rounds are built around a **lead investor** who commits first, sets the price
and terms, and does the legal work on the round documents. Once the lead is
committed, **followers** stack allocation into the same round on the same
terms without renegotiating.

Two implications founders often miss:

- **The lead is the single hardest yes to get, and everything else follows
  from it.** Most of your calendar effort should be aimed at converting a
  small number of realistic lead candidates, not spreading effort evenly
  across the whole list. A round is not raised until the lead signs; before
  that, every "verbal yes" is contingent.
- **Pre-seed rounds are often "party rounds"** raised on **SAFEs** with no
  formal lead — a stack of $50k–$250k checks from many angels and micro-funds
  that convert into equity at the next priced round. SAFEs, caps, discounts,
  and how they convert are covered in mod-005; here it is enough to know
  that a party round trades a lower-friction close for a diffuse cap table
  and a smaller total raise.

Seed rounds almost always have a priced lead, even if the round also stacks
SAFEs from other participants. If you cannot find a lead for a seed round,
that is a signal about the round's readiness, not a structural quirk to work
around.

### 7. Story vs. traction — what each stage buys

The same company gets pitched differently at each stage because different
things are for sale.

- **Pre-seed.** The bet is on the **founder and the wedge**. Investors are
  buying a person's ability to figure out an unsolved problem, and a
  plausible first customer to sell it to. What matters: your evidence from
  discovery (mod-001), the sharpness of the segment (mod-002), and the
  ambition of the eventual market.
- **Seed.** The bet is on the **story plus early evidence**. Something exists
  — an MVP, a design partner, a waitlist, a repeatable channel experiment.
  Enough that the investor can imagine what "it worked" would look like, but
  not so much that they need to see the numbers already at scale.
- **Series A.** The bet is on **repeatability**. Retention curves, cohort
  economics, a working acquisition channel with real payback. Series A
  investors underwrite the next 18 months of the operating plan, not the
  next chapter of the story.

A pre-seed pitch that leans on numbers usually falls flat because the numbers
are too small to be compelling; a Series A pitch that leans on the story
usually falls flat because the story cannot stand up to due-diligence
questions. Match the pitch to the stage.

### 8. Signal vs. theater in investor meetings

Investor politeness is a well-trained skill and can easily be mistaken for
interest. A useful discipline is to define, in advance, what a real signal
looks like and treat everything else as noise.

**Signals that a meeting went well:**

- A concrete second-meeting invite — a specific date, or a request to meet
  another partner *at* their firm.
- A reference call scheduled with a portfolio-company founder.
- A request to share the round's data room, deck, or specific diligence
  material.
- Detailed follow-up questions on model assumptions, unit economics, or
  hiring plans.

**Anti-signals dressed as compliments:**

- *"This was a great meeting; keep us posted as you make progress."* — a soft
  no.
- *"We're not ready to move but come back in three months."* — a no on this
  round; possibly a real yes on the next one if you deliver.
- *"We're excited but need to see [large, later-stage metric]."* — a no on
  this round; do not run your process around them.
- Radio silence for more than a week after a partner meeting — a no. The
  fund has decided; they simply have not told you yet.

The point of naming anti-signals is to keep the round's tracking sheet
honest. A pipeline that shows ten "in progress" conversations, seven of
which are actually soft no's, is a pipeline the founder is lying to
themselves about.

### 9. The close

A round closes when a lead signs the term sheet, the round paper (SAFE or
priced-round docs) is executed, and cash lands in the bank account. Between
term sheet and wire is typically **2–6 weeks** of diligence, legal, and
follow-on allocation. Two rules keep the close from slipping:

- **Do not stop the process at the term sheet.** Followers can and will
  disappear during the diligence gap. Keep the pipeline warm — keep taking
  meetings with credible followers — until the wire clears.
- **Bank the round in a scheduled window.** A four-week window with a hard
  close date focuses everyone. An open-ended "we're closing on a rolling
  basis" invites drift, gives followers no reason to move, and produces
  smaller rounds than the founder could have raised.

The very last discipline: once the round is closed, **email the passers**.
Investors remember the founders who keep them updated whether they said yes
or no; that list is the shortlist for the next round.

If you're an operating founder using this as a live lab: build the funnel
against your **actual** target list this week — the fund names, the specific
partners, the specific warm intros you can plausibly get. The scenario

real raise. Depend on mod-003: you raise against a model, not a vibe.

## Further reading

- Y Combinator, *A Guide to Seed Fundraising* (Geoff Ralston) — the canonical
  founder-facing overview of a seed round, from pipeline to close.
- Paul Graham, *How to Raise Money* (September 2013) and *How to Convince
  Investors* (August 2013) — the underlying model of parallel process,
  momentum, and what convinces investors at the earliest stages.
- Brad Feld & Jason Mendelson, *Venture Deals* — the mechanics of a round:
  term sheet clauses, dilution, control provisions, and what actually
  matters in the paper.
- Fred Wilson, *AVC* (avc.com) — twenty years of essays on the lead/follow
  structure, board dynamics, and what venture partners actually do.
- Elizabeth Yin, *The Funded* newsletter and *Democratizing Knowledge for
  Founders* posts — pipeline, conversion, and running a raise from the
  operator's side.
- Carta, *State of Private Markets* — quarterly primary data on check sizes,
  round sizes, valuations, and dilution at each stage.
