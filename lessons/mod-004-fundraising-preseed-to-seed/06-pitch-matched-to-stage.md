# Chapter 06 — Match the Pitch to the Stage: Founder-and-Wedge → Story-Plus-Evidence → Repeatability

> **Reads with:** module objective 6 — *match the pitch to the stage:
> pre-seed sells the founder-and-wedge, seed sells the story-plus-
> early-evidence, Series A sells repeatability (retention curves,
> cohort economics, working acquisition channel).*

## Different rungs price different things

Chapter 01 introduced the fundraising ladder as a set of distinct
markets. This chapter is about the corresponding shift in **what the
pitch has to be**. The same company gets pitched differently at each
stage because different things are for sale — and the most common
version of "our pitch isn't landing" is really a stage-mismatch
problem in disguise.

Three sentences to fix in your head before the rest of the chapter:

- **Pre-seed sells the founder and the wedge.**
- **Seed sells the story plus early evidence.**
- **Series A sells repeatability.**

A pre-seed pitch that leans on numbers usually falls flat because
the numbers are too small to be compelling; a Series A pitch that
leans on the story usually falls flat because the story cannot
stand up to due-diligence questions. Same company, wrong pitch —
the pass is structural.

## Pre-seed — the founder and the wedge

At pre-seed the investor is underwriting **a person's ability to
figure out an unsolved problem**, and **a specific first customer or
segment** to sell it to. There is often no product, sometimes no
co-founder, and usually no revenue. The pitch has to make the bet
on the founder plausible without pretending to be a bet on numbers.

What matters in a pre-seed pitch:

- **The founder's unfair understanding of the problem.** Where does
  your conviction come from — years working in the space, a specific
  personal experience, a research background, prior startup that
  bumped into this problem sideways? Mod-001 built this in the
  discovery work; the pitch is where you *use* it.
- **A sharp segment and a sharp wedge.** A specific first customer
  profile you can name, a specific first product cut you'd ship,
  and a specific first channel you'd use to reach them. Not "SMBs"
  and not "enterprise" — *"US-based cardiology practices with 3–8
  physicians using Epic Community Connect."*
- **A plausible bigger story around the wedge.** Where does the
  wedge lead in five years if it works? Investors at pre-seed are
  buying an early call option on the future company; the wedge has
  to open into something big enough to be worth their fund's
  attention.
- **Discovery evidence, not traction.** The 30 customer interviews
  from mod-001, the specific quotes, the specific willingness-to-pay
  signal, the specific person who said "I would sign up today."
  This is what stands in for the traction the pre-seed doesn't have
  yet.

What does *not* matter (yet):

- **Retention curves, cohort economics, working acquisition
  channels.** Series A material. Presenting week-8 retention on a
  32-user cohort at pre-seed reads as *"we don't have the story so
  we're leading with numbers that don't yet mean anything."*
- **Detailed financial models.** The mod-003 model exists — the
  investor may ask to see it in follow-up diligence — but it is not
  the pitch. A pre-seed partner is not underwriting your
  month-by-month burn projection.

The most common pre-seed pitching mistake is **pitching a seed
deck at pre-seed** — leading with the "10 paying customers" number
that hasn't happened yet, and burying the founder story that is
the real reason the round could close.

## Seed — the story plus early evidence

At seed the investor is underwriting **the story plus enough early
evidence that the story is real**. Something has to exist — an MVP,
a signed design partner, a repeatable channel experiment, a
waitlist with intent, a small paid pilot — enough that the
investor can imagine what *"it worked"* would look like.

What matters in a seed pitch:

- **The specific working thing.** An MVP that the partner can see,
  a paid pilot the partner can hear about from the customer, a
  design partner who will take a reference call. The seed round is
  not a promise of a wedge; it is a demonstration that the wedge
  exists in some form.
- **The next-round milestone.** The specific piece of
  investor-recognisable evidence the seed round is going to
  produce (see [chapter 02](./02-raise-amount-milestone-dilution.md)).
  *"In 15 months we will have 10 paying design partners at ≥ $3k
  ARPA / $360k ARR and demonstrated the sales cycle is under 6
  months."*
- **A credible team-and-plan for reaching the milestone.** The
  first hires, the go-to-market, the reason the founder is the
  person to build this. The mod-003 model is the exhibit that
  supports "we've thought about how the money gets spent."
- **A big-story frame.** The seed round is still story-heavy — the
  investor wants a Series-A-shaped narrative for what this could
  become, not just what it is today. But the story now has to
  hang off the evidence, not float on its own.
- **Early metrics if they exist and are honest.** Small numbers on
  small cohorts are fine — even valuable — if they're presented
  with the appropriate caveats. A 40% week-4 retention on a
  100-user cohort is a real signal at seed; presenting it as
  though it's a Series A retention curve is not.

What does *not* matter (yet):

- **Full cohort economics with statistical significance.** Series
  A material. At seed the partner accepts that the sample size is
  small and looks for direction, not a defensible curve.
- **A working outbound engine you can turn a budget dial on.**
  Series A material. At seed a channel *experiment* that suggests
  the outbound engine could work is enough.

The most common seed pitching mistake is either direction:
**pitching a pre-seed deck at seed** (founder-and-wedge story with
no evidence, when the market now wants some evidence) or
**pitching a Series A deck at seed** (heavy on metrics that are
neither large enough nor statistically defensible enough to survive
the pressure).

## Series A — repeatability

At Series A the investor is underwriting **repeatability**. The
question is no longer *"can this be a company?"* but *"can this be
a machine we underwrite the next round of?"* — and the machine has
to be visible in the numbers.

What matters in a Series A pitch:

- **Retention curves.** Cohort retention over a meaningful time
  horizon — weekly for consumer, monthly or contract-cycle for
  B2B. The curve has to flatten to a level that supports the LTV
  the company depends on. A curve that decays to zero is a
  company that has no LTV.

- **Cohort economics.** For each cohort of acquired customers:
  what did they cost to acquire (CAC), what have they paid so far,
  what is the projected LTV, and how long is the payback window.
  Investors want to see this by cohort, not just as company
  averages that hide the truth.
- **A working acquisition channel.** At least one channel the
  founder can honestly say "we spend $X and get $Y in new ARR
  back in Z months." The channel doesn't need to be the only one
  or the biggest one; it needs to be **real, repeatable, and
  scalable with more budget**.
- **A believable operating plan for the next 18 months.** The
  mod-003 model, now with real inputs — real revenue curve, real
  hiring plan, real spend against real channels — showing what the
  Series A dollars buy in the language of the Series B milestone.
- **A team that can execute at the next stage.** Named early
  hires, gaps identified, an honest read on what management the
  company needs to add and when.

What matters less (but still matters):

- **The story.** Not gone — Series A partners still want to
  understand the big vision — but the story now supports the
  numbers rather than substituting for them. A great story with a
  broken retention curve does not raise a Series A.
- **The founder narrative.** Still there, but the investor is now
  betting on the founder-plus-machine, not the founder as call
  option.

The most common Series A pitching mistake is **pitching a seed
deck at Series A** — leaning on the story with hand-waved metrics,
when the partner is trying to underwrite the retention and the
CAC-payback numbers that the seed round was supposed to produce.

## A one-page contrast

| Dimension | Pre-seed | Seed | Series A |
|---|---|---|---|
| What's being bought | Founder + wedge | Story + early evidence | Repeatability (retention, cohort economics, working channel) |
| Product state | Idea, prototype, sometimes nothing | MVP, working pilot, signed design partner | Live, in-market, growing |
| Evidence needed | Discovery interviews, unfair knowledge | Small cohorts, first paying users, channel experiments | Cohort retention curves, cohort LTV/CAC, repeatable channel |
| Team state | Founder + maybe co-founder | Founder + first hires | Small management team, plan for next hires |
| Financial model | Sketch, back-of-envelope | mod-003 model with defended assumptions | Operating plan tied to Series B milestone, cohort-based inputs |
| Story weight | High | Medium — story with early proof | Lower — story frames the numbers, not vice versa |
| Numbers weight | Low | Medium | High — the numbers are the pitch |

## Adjusting the deck vs. adjusting the pitch

Two operational moves fall out of this chapter.

- **Have a stage-appropriate deck.** A single deck used across
  stages is a deck that is wrong for at least two of the three
  stages. If you're raising a seed, the pre-seed version of your
  deck (founder-first, wedge-first) is the wrong document; if
  you're raising a Series A, the seed version of your deck
  (story-first, small-cohort metrics) is the wrong document.
- **Adjust the pitch per meeting when you can tell you're in the
  wrong-stage room.** Sometimes a Series A partner takes a seed
  meeting to build the relationship, or a seed fund partner takes
  a pre-seed meeting for a founder they know. In both cases,
  match the pitch to the partner's actual underwriting frame —
  not the round you *want* them to price.

## The four common wrong versions

- **Pre-seed pitched as a small seed.** Leading with tiny metrics
  that don't yet mean anything, burying the founder-and-wedge
  story that is the real reason for the round.
- **Seed pitched as a big pre-seed.** All story, no evidence, in a
  market that now expects the evidence. The partner leaves
  wondering what the last 12 months were spent building.
- **Series A pitched as a big seed.** Heavy on the story, thin on
  the retention curves and cohort economics, in a market that
  reads that as *"we know the numbers don't support the round."*
- **Same deck across all stages.** The deck can't tell whether
  the founder is asking for $1M or $15M, and the partner reading
  it can't either. Both stages of investor pass for structurally
  different reasons.

## Summary

- The same company pitches differently at different stages.
  **Pre-seed sells the founder and the wedge; seed sells the story
  plus early evidence; Series A sells repeatability.**
- What "evidence" means shifts by stage — from discovery
  interviews (pre-seed) to small cohorts and design partners
  (seed) to cohort retention curves and repeatable channels
  (Series A).
- The pitch document should shift with the stage, not just the
  ask. A single all-stage deck is the wrong deck for at least two
  of the three stages.
- Adjust to the partner in the room. If you can tell you're in a
  wrong-stage meeting, match their underwriting frame rather than
  the round you want them to price.
