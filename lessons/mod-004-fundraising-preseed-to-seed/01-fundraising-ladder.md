# Chapter 01 — The Fundraising Ladder: Angels → Pre-seed → Seed → Series A

> **Reads with:** module objective 1 — *describe the fundraising ladder
> — angels ($10k–$100k individual checks) → pre-seed ($500k–$3M from
> pre-seed funds) → seed ($2M–$5M traditional, $5M–$15M 'big seed') →
> Series A ($8M–$25M) — including what each stage expects from the
> founder in exchange.*

## Rungs, not a spectrum

"Fundraising" is not one market. It is a **ladder** of distinct
markets, each with its own investors, check sizes, expectations, and
paperwork. Pitching a pre-seed round to a Series A partner wastes both
parties' time and burns a warm intro; pitching a Series A story with
pre-seed evidence gets you a polite pass. Founders who lose weeks to
avoidable mismatches almost always did so because they treated the
whole venture ecosystem as one blurred audience.

Every rung answers the same three questions, differently:

- **Who writes the check?** Individual, micro-fund, seed fund,
  multi-stage fund.
- **What is being priced?** A person and a wedge, a story with early
  evidence, or a repeatable machine.
- **What does the founder trade in exchange?** Dilution, board
  structure, reporting cadence, and the milestone the round buys.

The ranges in the rest of this chapter are US-market norms across
roughly the last several years. They shift year to year and vary by
geography and sector; treat them as the shape of the ladder, not as
lookup values.

## Rung 0 — Friends, family, and angels

**Individual checks of roughly $10k–$100k** from people investing
their own money.

- **Who.** Personal network, industry angels, operator-angels who back
  founders in a domain they know. Sometimes syndicated through
  AngelList or a rolling fund.
- **What they buy.** Belief in the founder — often on nothing more
  than the idea and a demo. Diligence is light; the conversation is
  measured in a coffee, not a partner meeting.
- **What they expect back.** A SAFE, sometimes a note. No board seat,
  no observer rights, no formal reporting cadence. Most angels expect
  quarterly updates because they want them, not because a document
  requires them.
- **What it's for.** Bridging from idea to a fundable pre-seed round —
  paying for the first prototype, the first month of a co-founder's
  salary, the first ten customer discovery interviews. Also useful to
  **fill out** a later round once a lead has priced it.

Angel money moves fast — days, not months — but is expensive per
dollar raised in **founder attention**. Ten $25k angel checks is ten
separate reference calls, ten separate SAFEs, and ten quarterly
updates. Past a certain count, the diligence overhead exceeds the
value of the incremental angel and you should be raising the next
round instead.

## Rung 1 — Pre-seed

**Roughly $500k–$3M**, typically on SAFEs, often as a "party round"
of stacked checks with no formal lead (see
[chapter 05](./05-lead-follow-vs-party-round.md)).

- **Who.** Institutional pre-seed funds (Hustle Fund, Precursor, K9,
  and a growing category of others), specialised operator-angels
  syndicating on AngelList, occasionally a seed fund taking an early
  position to build a relationship before pricing at seed.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the roster of active pre-seed funds; the specific funds named here should be verified against each fund's current stated stage focus. -->
- **What they buy.** The **founder and a plausible wedge**. There is
  often no product and no revenue — occasionally not even a
  co-founder. What they underwrite is the founder's ability to figure
  out an unsolved problem, and a specific first customer or segment
  to sell it to. See [chapter 06](./06-pitch-matched-to-stage.md) for
  what this means for the pitch.
- **What they expect back.** SAFE paperwork; a valuation *cap* rather
  than a priced round in most cases (mod-005 covers the mechanics).
  Rarely a board seat. Quarterly updates are the norm — the fund
  wants signal on whether the wedge is turning into evidence.
- **What it's for.** Buying **12–18 months of runway** to prove the
  wedge is real — first users, first design partner, first pricing
  experiment, first indication that the segment behaves the way the
  canvas said it would.

The most common pre-seed mistake is treating it like a small seed. A
pre-seed round is priced on a person and an insight; if the fund
wanted retention curves and cohort economics they would be doing a
seed round instead. Skipping the pre-seed and going straight to seed
is a legitimate choice when the founder brings enough evidence with
them (a prior exit, a strong customer letter, a working paid pilot);
skipping it because "we need more money" is usually a sign the story
is not yet a seed story.

## Rung 2 — Seed

**Roughly $2M–$5M at the traditional end and $5M–$15M** at the larger
"seed extension" or "big seed" end that has become common.

- **Who.** Seed funds (First Round, Uncork, Founder Collective, and a
  long tail of others), plus multi-stage funds writing early checks.
  A seed round almost always has a **priced lead** — the fund that
  sets the price, signs the term sheet, and does the round paperwork.
  See [chapter 05](./05-lead-follow-vs-party-round.md) for the
  lead-follow structure.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the roster of active seed funds; the specific funds named here should be verified against each fund's current stated stage focus. -->
- **What they buy.** The **story plus early evidence**. Something has
  to exist — an MVP, a signed design partner, a repeatable channel
  experiment, a waitlist with intent, a small paid pilot. Enough that
  the investor can imagine what "it worked" would look like without
  needing to see the numbers already at scale.
- **What they expect back.** Preferred shares (typically a
  YC-standard series or a lightweight seed-preferred), a **board
  seat** for the lead, monthly or quarterly reporting, standard
  protective provisions on the round documents.
  <!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the "board seat for the lead at seed" norm — Y Combinator's *A Guide to Seed Fundraising* and Brad Feld / Jason Mendelson's *Venture Deals* both cover this, but norms vary by round size and fund. Verify current practice against Carta *State of Private Markets*. -->
- **What it's for.** Buying **18–24 months of runway** to reach the
  evidence a Series A investor will underwrite — retention, cohort
  economics, a working acquisition channel. The milestone the seed
  round buys is what raises the Series A; a seed round that lands
  without a Series-A-shaped milestone in view is a seed round that
  will need a bridge.

The "big seed" phenomenon deserves a callout. Seed rounds have grown
larger in recent cycles as multi-stage funds have moved earlier and
some founders raise more up front to reduce the number of rounds they
need to run. A $10M seed is not "just a bigger seed" — it is a round
that sets a valuation the Series A will have to grow into, and it
implies a bigger milestone (mod-005 covers the arithmetic; the
dilution and next-round pricing are non-trivial). Bigger is not
always better.

## Rung 3 — Series A

**Roughly $8M–$25M** from a lead partner at a multi-stage fund
(Sequoia, Benchmark, a16z, Accel, Greylock, Kleiner, and peers).
<!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the Series A check-size and round-size ranges; recent PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor and Carta *State of Private Markets* reports are the current empirical anchors. -->

- **Who.** Multi-stage funds where a single partner will take a
  **board seat** and run point on the company for years. Series A is
  the round where the fund's brand and the specific partner both
  matter — you are hiring a board member as much as raising money.
- **What they buy.** **Repeatability.** Retention curves that a
  cohort analysis can defend, unit economics that pay back the CAC in
  a plausible window, an acquisition channel that works at some
  budget the company can honestly step up. See
  [chapter 06](./06-pitch-matched-to-stage.md) for what this means
  for the pitch. Series A investors underwrite the *next* 18 months
  of the operating plan, not the next chapter of the story.
- **What they expect back.** A priced preferred-stock round, a board
  seat for the lead, standard protective provisions, information
  rights, and pro-rata rights. Monthly reporting is the norm. The
  paperwork gets meaningfully heavier than seed; the diligence
  process is longer.
- **What it's for.** Buying **18–24 months of runway** to reach the
  scale a Series B investor will underwrite — a real revenue number,
  a real customer count, evidence that the acquisition machine
  continues to work as the budget is turned up. The founder trades
  more control and more dilution in exchange for a partner who is
  supposed to be at the table for the next several years.

Series A is deliberately named as the top rung of *this* module
because the fundraising process craft above Series A — Series B and
beyond, secondary transactions, growth rounds, PIPE and crossover
capital, IPO readiness — is a permanent CFO-and-CEO discipline that
this module does not try to teach. See
[chapter 09](./09-boundaries-finance-and-governance.md) for the
handoff to the `startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum` peer track.

## What each rung expects from the founder in exchange

The check size is the loud number; the *trades* are the ones founders
under-anticipate.

| Rung        | Typical instrument       | Board seat            | Reporting cadence     | Founder trade beyond dilution |
|-------------|---------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|-------------------------------|
| Angel       | SAFE                      | No                    | Optional / ad-hoc     | Time on reference calls, warm-intro reciprocity |
| Pre-seed    | SAFE (cap, sometimes discount) | Rare               | Quarterly             | Written milestones the fund will hold you to |
| Seed        | Preferred stock (priced)  | Yes — lead            | Monthly / quarterly   | A board that meets, protective provisions, information rights |
| Series A    | Preferred stock (priced)  | Yes — lead + often independent | Monthly + board deck | A partner in the strategy, hiring input, governance discipline |

## Why the rungs go together

Every round is expected to buy roughly **12–24 months of runway** and
produce enough evidence to justify the next round at a higher
valuation. If a round doesn't do both — or if the founder cannot
sketch a plausible story for the next round while pitching this one —
the round after it will be a **bridge** or a **down round**, and both
are strictly worse outcomes than a clean step up.
<!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the 12–24 month post-raise runway norm; commonly repeated in the Y Combinator *A Guide to Seed Fundraising* (Geoff Ralston) and Sequoia writings, exact origin varies. -->

The practical corollary: when you are raising a pre-seed, you should
already have the *shape* of the seed story in mind ("in 18 months we
will have signed 10 design partners and be at $1M ARR"); when you are
raising a seed, you should already have the *shape* of the Series A
story in mind ("in 18 months we will have $3M ARR with a working
outbound channel and > 100% net revenue retention"). The rungs go
together; skipping the framing on the current round leaves the next
one un-buildable.

## The four common wrong versions

- **Wrong rung.** Pitching a pre-seed to a Series A partner, or a
  Series A to a seed fund. Both waste warm intros and mark the
  founder as un-oriented to the market.
- **"Amount without a milestone."** Asking for "$3M" without naming
  the milestone the $3M buys. See
  [chapter 02](./02-raise-amount-milestone-dilution.md); this is one
  of the most common seed-round failures.
- **Skipping the pre-seed with a pre-seed story.** Raising a seed
  round on wedge-level evidence and then failing to grow into the
  valuation. Better to raise a smaller round at a lower cap and
  return in 12–18 months with real evidence.
- **Treating angels as a substitute for a lead.** A party round of
  angels can bridge you to a seed; it cannot substitute for a
  priced seed lead. If your seed round has no lead, that is a signal
  about readiness, not a structural quirk to work around. See
  [chapter 05](./05-lead-follow-vs-party-round.md).

## Summary

- Fundraising is a **ladder**: angels ($10k–$100k), pre-seed
  ($500k–$3M), seed ($2M–$5M traditional / $5M–$15M "big seed"),
  Series A ($8M–$25M).
- Each rung has different investors, different instruments (SAFE vs
  priced preferred), different governance expectations (no seat →
  lead seat → lead + independent), and buys a different thing
  (belief → wedge → story + evidence → repeatability).
- Every round is a purchase of roughly **12–24 months of runway** and
  must produce enough evidence to justify the *next* round at a step
  up. If it doesn't, the next round is a bridge or a down round.
- Match the pitch, the paperwork, and the milestone to the rung.
  Mismatches are the most common reason a technically-fundable
  company burns warm intros on the wrong process.
