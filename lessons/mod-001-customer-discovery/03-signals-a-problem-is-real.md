# Chapter 03 — The Four Signals a Problem Is Real

> **Reads with:** module objective 3 — *recognise the four signals that a
> problem is real: prior spend, a specific recent story, quantified cost, and
> an existing hacky workaround.*

## Why signals, not opinions

Discovery interviews return two kinds of content: **what the buyer thinks**
and **what the buyer has done**. Only the second is worth much. Opinions are
free to give, cheap to change, and unpredictive of purchase behavior. Past
behavior — where the buyer already spent time, money, or attention — is the
closest thing an interview can give you to a demand signal short of a sale.

Chapter 02 covered how to elicit that behavior. This chapter covers what to
listen *for*. If your interview notes are lit up with the four signals below,
you are looking at a candidate real problem. If they aren't, you are almost
certainly looking at a nice-to-have — and nice-to-haves don't fund companies.

## Signal 1 — Prior spend (time or money)

The strongest signal a problem is real is that the buyer has *already spent
something on it*. Money is the cleanest form: they hired a consultant, bought
a template, paid for a competing tool, put an internal analyst on it,
subscribed to a service that half-solves it. Time counts too, if it's
substantial and repeatable: a founder rebuilding the same operating dashboard
every quarter, an ops lead spending Fridays reconciling a spreadsheet, a
recruiter running the same manual sourcing loop.

Why it's the strongest signal: money and calendar are the scarcest resources
inside any organisation. Anything already claiming them has passed a filter
that a "sounds interesting" response has not.

What to listen for:

- Named vendors, contract sizes, subscription lines. ("We pay a fractional
  CFO $3k/month partly for this.")
- Internal headcount pointed at the problem. ("Our office manager runs
  this every Monday.")
- Repeated purchases. One-time purchases are experiments; renewals are
  demand.
- Explicit "I'd pay for" statements *only when they're anchored to something
  the buyer already pays for* — otherwise treat as opinion.

What is *not* prior spend: a free trial that lapsed, a bookmarked article,
"I've been meaning to look into it." All of those are curiosity, not
commitment.

## Signal 2 — A specific recent story

A real problem produces stories with detail: dates, people, dollar amounts,
what was said in the meeting, what got emailed on Thursday. A fake problem
produces generalities: "yeah, it's always kind of a mess," "we struggle with
that sometimes."

The interview move to force the distinction is the past-tense follow-up
(chapter 02): *"tell me about the last time."* If the buyer can walk you
through a specific instance in the last month or two — with the kind of
detail a person only has because it actually happened — the problem is
episodic and recent enough to be a purchase candidate. If they can't, either
the problem doesn't occur often enough to matter, or they don't personally
feel it (which usually means you're talking to the wrong person; see
chapter 04).

What to write down: the story itself, in the buyer's own words where you can
manage. In the synthesis stage, a story you can quote is worth ten
paraphrased summaries.

## Signal 3 — A quantified cost

Real problems have a cost the buyer can name, at least approximately, in a
unit they care about. Common shapes:

- **Money.** Deals lost, penalties incurred, over-hires made, dilutive terms
  taken (a bad SAFE, an oversized option pool), refunds paid, contracts torn
  up.
- **Time.** Hours per week per person, weeks of a project delay, a month
  chasing a partnership that couldn't close.
- **Headcount.** A person hired mostly to work around the problem.
- **Opportunity.** A whole segment or channel abandoned because the buyer
  couldn't operate in it.

The cost does not have to be precise. It has to be *nameable*. "I think it
cost us about two months and a hire I regret" is a signal; "it's really
inefficient" is not.

A related move is asking the buyer to estimate the cost *relative* to
something they already spend on: "compared to what you pay in
[recruiter fees / cloud / legal], is this bigger or smaller?" Relative
anchoring gets a usable estimate out of a buyer who couldn't name an
absolute number.

## Signal 4 — An existing hacky workaround

If the buyer is already doing something ugly to cope, the problem is
real *and* they are the buyer. Workarounds you should be listening for:

- **Spreadsheets doing a job they were never designed for.** The single most
  reliable indicator of an unmet need inside a serious operating context.
- **Zapier / no-code chains** wiring three tools together to fake a fourth.
- **A junior person doing a task manually every week** that everyone knows
  should be automated.
- **A competitor product misused** — a CRM used as a project tracker, a
  Notion database as a CRM. Misuse reveals demand shape.
- **Internal scripts, cron jobs, or Airtables** propping up something
  business-critical.

Workarounds are load-bearing evidence for a specific reason: someone paid
the cost to build and maintain them. That cost is the buyer's own proof
the pain justified an investment — the strongest analog of "willingness to
pay" you can get from a person who hasn't yet paid you.

Listen for the *emotional* colour around a workaround too. A workaround the
buyer is proud of ("we hacked this together and it works great") is not the
same signal as one they resent ("I hate that this is still my problem every
Monday"). Resentment is the tell that the buyer would trade the workaround
for a real solution today.

## How the four signals combine

No single signal is decisive. The pattern that matters is *co-occurrence*:

- Prior spend + a workaround + resentment + a quantified cost → you have a
  real, funded, urgent problem. This is the shape a first customer looks
  like.
- Prior spend + no workaround + no story → probably a whitespace category
  where the buyer is guessing what they need. Interview more, don't build.
- Story + cost + no prior spend + no workaround → the pain exists but the
  buyer isn't yet motivated enough to act. The problem is real but not
  urgent; you're a nice-to-have.
- Compliments + enthusiasm + no signals → politeness. Do not build.

In the synthesis stage (chapter 05), you'll **count** how many interviews
produced each of the four signals *unprompted*. That count is the honest
version of "we validated the problem." One or two signals across the whole
set is a kill; three of four in a majority of the set is a strong go.

## The absence check

The complement of the four signals is a useful heuristic on its own. When
none appears — no prior spend, no specific story, no quantified cost, no
workaround — you are almost certainly listening to a **nice-to-have**. The
buyer is being polite. Something they would be pleased to have is not
something they'd change vendors, sign a contract, or pull a purchase order
for. Nice-to-haves are the most expensive category of feedback to
misinterpret because they *sound* like validation.

The disciplined move when the signals are absent is not to reword the
question and try again. It is to write down "no signals from this segment"
and either move to a new segment or park the hypothesis.

## Summary

- Discovery listens for four behavioral signals: **prior spend**, a
  **specific recent story**, a **quantified cost**, and an existing
  **hacky workaround**.
- Signals co-occur. The count of how many interviews independently surfaced
  each — unprompted — is the honest metric for "is this problem real."
- The absence of all four is the shape of a nice-to-have. Nice-to-haves do
  not become companies; treat their absence as a discovery result, not a
  reason to try harder to reframe the question.
