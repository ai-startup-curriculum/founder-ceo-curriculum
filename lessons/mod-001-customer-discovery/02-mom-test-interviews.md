# Chapter 02 — Mom Test Interviews: Behavior over Politeness

> **Reads with:** module objective 2 — *recruit and run customer interviews
> that surface behavior and evidence, not politeness and speculation, using
> the Mom Test discipline — past-tense, non-leading, behavioral.*

## What the Mom Test actually is

Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013) is a discipline for asking questions
whose answers you can trust — questions you could ask even your mother without
her flattering you into a false yes. It is not a script; it is three habits
about how you talk to a would-be customer.

The three rules, in Fitzpatrick's own framing:
<!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the three Mom Test rules — Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test: How to talk to customers & learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you* (2013), chapter 1. -->

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.**
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the
   future.**
3. **Talk less and listen more.**

Each rule exists because a specific failure mode is otherwise nearly
automatic:

- Talk about the idea and the interview becomes a pitch. The buyer politely
  helps you feel good about it. You collect compliments and interpret them
  as demand.
- Ask about the future ("would you use…?") and you get speculation — the
  buyer imagines a better version of themselves. Humans predict their future
  behavior badly and generously.
- Talk more than half the time and you fill the silence with your framing.
  The interview becomes an echo chamber.

The Mom Test's contribution is that it makes these failure modes *inspectable
per question*: any single question in your guide either passes the three
rules or it doesn't.

## The behavioral rewrite

The single most useful move is to rewrite every hypothetical or leading
question as a **past-tense, behavioral** version.

| Bad (hypothetical / leading) | Good (behavioral, past-tense) |
|---|---|
| "Would you use a tool that helps you onboard faster?" | "Walk me through the last time you onboarded a new hire. What actually took the time?" |
| "How important is data quality to you?" | "Tell me about the last time you found a data quality problem. What did you do about it?" |
| "Do you think you'd pay for that?" | "What have you already bought or built to solve this problem?" |
| "Would this save you time?" | "How long did that take you last week? Who was involved?" |

The right-hand column has three properties the left column doesn't:

- It refers to something that actually happened, so the answer is
  observation rather than prediction.
- It is unfalsifiable to please the interviewer — the buyer can't invent a
  flattering story without visibly doing so.
- It surfaces the *signals* chapter 03 covers (prior spend, story detail,
  cost, workaround) as a matter of course, because those are exactly what
  behavioral questions elicit.

## Recruiting: who to talk to, and how to ask

Recruiting is a filter on the population; the segment definition (chapter 04)
is the filter. Once the filter is set, the recruit ask is short:

> "I'm trying to understand what the first six months of running a company
> are actually like — I'm not selling anything. Could you spare 20–30 minutes
> to walk me through yours?"

Two properties of that ask matter:

- **No pitch.** The purpose is stated as learning, not selling. If your
  outreach mentions your product, you've traded a good interview for a bad
  demo before it started.
- **Time-boxed and specific.** "20–30 minutes to walk me through" is
  concrete; "pick your brain" is not, and its response rate is worse for the
  same reason.

Recruit channels, in typical yield order:

- Warm intros from investors, advisors, prior colleagues — high fit, low
  friction.
- Founder / operator communities (relevant Slack and Discord servers,
  segment-specific forums).
- Cold outreach against a public signal (a recent raise announcement, a
  hiring post, a conference talk). Reference the signal in the first line.

For pass-rates: assume half the people who reply "yes" won't take the call,
and half of the calls that happen won't yield a usable interview. Recruit
against a 4× multiple of your target interview count.

## Running the interview: shape and moves

A 30-minute interview has a rough shape:

- **First 2–3 minutes** — restate the "I'm not selling anything" framing,
  give the buyer a minute to describe themselves and their role. This is not
  filler; it calibrates what "recently" means for them.
- **Next 15–20 minutes** — walk the past. Open questions from your guide, in
  the order that follows the buyer's story, not the order in your document.
  When the buyer says something emotional or absolute ("that was a
  nightmare," "we don't do that anymore"), stop and dig: *when was that?
  what happened? what did you do?*
- **Last 5–7 minutes** — hand off the mic. "What haven't I asked that I
  should have?" and "Who else should I talk to?" Both surface information
  your guide couldn't have anticipated.

Two moves worth practicing:

- **The follow-up loop.** Any generic answer ("that was hard") gets one
  follow-up: *"tell me about the last time."* Any quantified claim ("we
  waste hours a week") gets one follow-up: *"how do you know? what were
  you doing when you noticed?"* Two loops per emotional beat is a good
  target.
- **The silence pause.** After the buyer finishes an answer, wait three
  seconds before speaking. The most useful thing they say often comes after
  what they thought was the end.

## What to write down (and what not to)

Capture, per interview:

- Segment fit (one line — do they match the segment definition?).
- The specific recent story, with dates or approximate dates.
- What they've already tried or paid for, and roughly how much.
- The concrete cost of the pain, if any (money, time, headcount, deal lost).
- Surprises — anything that contradicted your prior expectation.

Do not capture:

- Your interpretation of what the story "means." Interpretations belong in
  the synthesis stage (chapter 05), where they can be checked against
  patterns across the whole set.
- The buyer's advice about your product. It's the wrong artifact for the
  wrong stage; you didn't come to demo.

## The compliments trap

The single most common failure of a Mom-Test-uninformed interview is
misreading compliments as evidence. "That sounds really useful," "I'd
definitely use that," and "you should build that" are almost always
politeness, not commitment. Fitzpatrick's rule of thumb is that the interview
should end with the buyer either committing something concrete — an
introduction, a follow-up meeting, time to try a prototype, a small payment
— or with information about their past behavior. Everything else is
conversation, and conversation is free.

## Summary

- The Mom Test is three habits: talk about their life, ask about the past,
  talk less than half the time.
- The single most valuable move is rewriting every hypothetical into a
  past-tense behavioral question that would still make sense if the buyer
  had never heard of you.
- Recruit for narrow segments with a short, non-pitch, time-boxed ask;
  assume ~4× replies to get to your target interview count.
- Compliments are not evidence. Behavior is.
