# Exercise 03 — Segment-Narrowing Drill

**Module:** 001 Customer Discovery · **Stage:** IDEA→PRE-SEED · **Time:**
~90 focused minutes · **Fits before:**
[exercise-02](./exercise-02-mom-test-interview-guide-authoring.md) and
[exercise-01](./exercise-01-discovery-sprint.md).

## Problem statement

Almost every failed first discovery sprint fails at the same step: the
segment was too broad. "Founders," "SMBs," "marketers" — each is a
population large enough that any real pattern gets averaged into
invisibility. This exercise is the drill that makes you narrow a broad
segment name down, in explicit iterations, until the segment is one
recognisable sentence that you can name three real people for and know
how to reach twenty more.

Ninety minutes here saves a week of wasted interviews later. Do this
exercise *before*
[exercise-02](./exercise-02-mom-test-interview-guide-authoring.md); the
interview guide's questions depend on knowing who is answering them.

## Deliverable (one artifact)

A short **segment narrowing worksheet** (one page) with:

- Your **starting broad segment name** (the one you would have said out
  loud a week ago).
- **Three or more explicit narrowing iterations** (v1 → v2 → v3 → …),
  each showing the axis you added and the axis you rejected.
- The **final one-sentence segment definition** — the filter you'd apply
  to LinkedIn, warm intros, and community posts.
- **Three real, named people** (or, on the simulated path, three
  archetypes with the specific channels you'd find them through) who
  fit the final definition.
- A **recognisability check** and a **reachability check** — one
  sentence each — arguing that the segment is externally observable and
  that you could plausibly reach 15–20 members through the channels
  listed.

## Scenario

Pick one:

- **Your real startup**: use the segment you'd currently, in your head,
  point discovery at. If you don't have one, take the broadest audience
  name you'd say if asked "who is this for?"
- **Simulated:** start from *"founders"* as the broad segment for the
  module's exemplar problem hypothesis (see
  [exercise-01](./exercise-01-discovery-sprint.md)) and narrow from
  there.

Either way, the exercise is *not* to arrive at the exemplar's segment.
It's to demonstrate that you can iterate, with reasons, from a broad
name to a recognisable one.

## Requirements

The worksheet has to satisfy each of the following:

- **At least three narrowing iterations.** One-shot "here's my narrow
  segment" doesn't teach the muscle; the iterations do.
- **Each iteration names its axis explicitly.** Options include stage,
  recency of a triggering situation, experience (first-time vs.
  second-time), team shape (solo / small team / mid-market / enterprise),
  buying authority (user vs. buyer), industry, geography, sales cycle
  shape. See [chapter 04](../04-sizing-and-segmenting.md) for the axes
  that most reliably matter.
- **Each iteration names one axis rejected too**, with a one-sentence
  reason. Rejection is as informative as selection.
- **Final segment is one sentence.** If it takes a paragraph, keep
  cutting. Two clauses maximum.
- **Recognisability**: you can state, in one sentence, how you'd tell
  from someone's public presence (LinkedIn, a raise announcement, a
  conference talk, a job title, a public post) whether they're in the
  segment.
- **Reachability**: you can list at least three channels (warm-intro
  paths, communities, public-signal cold outreach) through which you
  could plausibly reach 15–20 members inside a two-week window.
- **Three named people (or three concrete archetypes with channels)**
  who fit the final definition. If you can't name any, the segment is
  either too abstract or so narrow it doesn't exist as a reachable
  population — go back and re-narrow.

## Starter guidance

**Step 1 — Read chapter 04's segmenting section.** Especially the axes-that-
matter list. Five minutes.

**Step 2 — Write the broad starting name.** One line. Resist the urge to
narrow before you've written the "before" version — you want the
audit trail.

**Step 3 — Iterate.** For each round:
- Pick one axis to add (from the list in chapter 04 or your own).
- Restate the segment with the new axis included.
- Sanity-check: does this axis actually correlate with the pain you're
  testing? If not, back it out.
- Pick one axis you *considered* but rejected, and write one sentence
  on why (usually "not correlated with the pain," "not observable,"
  or "not reachable at 15–20 scale").

**Step 4 — Stop at recognisable + reachable.** The stopping condition
is not "as narrow as possible." It is: **can I recognise a member from
public signals, and can I reach 15–20 of them in two weeks?** Go one
axis past that condition and you'll have a segment that's real but too
small to interview.

**Step 5 — Name three people.** If step 4 landed in the right place,
this is easy. If it's hard, the segment is still too abstract; add
another axis or replace one you rejected.

**Step 6 — Write the recognisability and reachability sentences.**
Explicitly. These are the answers to the two questions a co-founder
would ask you first.

## Acceptance criteria

You are done when *all* of the following are true:

- [ ] The worksheet shows **at least three explicit iterations** from a
      broad starting name to a narrow final definition.
- [ ] Each iteration **names both the axis added and an axis rejected**,
      with a one-sentence reason for the rejection.
- [ ] The final segment is **one sentence, two clauses at most**.
- [ ] You have written a **recognisability sentence** and can defend it
      against "how would a stranger tell?"
- [ ] You have listed **at least three channels** with a rough count
      each, and the totals plausibly cover a 15–20-interview sprint
      inside two weeks (see [chapter 04](../04-sizing-and-segmenting.md)'s
      recruit funnel arithmetic).
- [ ] You can name **three real people (or three concrete archetypes
      with channels)** who fit the final definition.
- [ ] The final segment is **not "founders," "SMBs," "marketers," or any
      other undifferentiated population name**.

## Self-assessment rubric

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Iterations | One jump from broad to narrow | 3+ explicit rounds, each with an added and a rejected axis |
| Final sentence | Paragraph-length, hedged | One sentence, two clauses maximum |
| Axis choice | Purely demographic (industry, size) | Axes correlated with the pain (recency, experience, team shape, buying authority) |
| Recognisability | "You just know" | Externally observable signal named (LinkedIn, raise, job title, community post) |
| Reachability | "My network" as one line | 3+ channels named with plausible per-channel counts |
| Named people | None | Three real names or three concrete archetypes with channels |
| Stopping | Segment so narrow it can't be reached | Narrow enough to be homogeneous, wide enough to reach 15–20 in two weeks |

## Definition of done

A co-founder can read the worksheet, disagree with your segment, and
their disagreement is about *which axis you rejected* — not "who exactly
are we talking about?" That means the segment is specific and defensible
enough to run [exercise-01's discovery sprint](./exercise-01-discovery-sprint.md)
against. If they instead ask "wait, what does that mean?", keep
iterating.
