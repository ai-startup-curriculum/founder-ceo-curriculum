# Chapter 01 — Problem Hypothesis vs Solution Hypothesis

> **Reads with:** module objective 1 — *distinguish a problem hypothesis from a
> solution hypothesis, and know which one discovery tests first.*

## Why this comes first

Founders arrive at discovery with a bundle they call "the idea." Unpacked, that
bundle is almost always two separate bets glued together:

1. A **problem hypothesis** — a claim about a specific person, in a specific
   situation, experiencing a specific pain badly enough to act on it.
2. A **solution hypothesis** — a claim about *what you would build* to relieve
   that pain and *why that shape* is the right one.

Confusing the two is the most common early-stage error. If you test the
solution first — demoing a mockup, asking "would you use this?" — a warm
response tells you nothing you can bank on. People are generous about products
that don't exist yet and are being described by their inventor. Worse: a
positive response on the solution *feels like* validation of the underlying
problem, and quiet, expensive commitments follow.

Steve Blank's customer-development model, on which most modern startup
discovery is built, treats these as two sequential activities: **customer
discovery** (is there a problem worth solving, and is there a segment that
knows they have it?) precedes **customer validation** (does our solution shape
actually get bought?). Discovery does not test the solution.
<!-- needs-research: primary-source citation for the customer-development sequence — Steve Blank, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005) and *The Startup Owner's Manual* (2012). -->

The Lean Startup vocabulary — Eric Ries, 2011 — separates the same idea into
**value hypothesis** (is anyone willing to trade something for the outcome?)
and **growth hypothesis** (does it spread?). The value hypothesis is what a
first discovery sprint is trying to falsify.

## How to state each one so it's testable

A well-formed problem hypothesis has four visible parts. If any is missing,
the hypothesis is a slogan, not a bet you can lose.

- **Who** — the segment, narrowly enough that you can name three real people
  in it (see chapter 04).
- **When** — the situation or trigger that surfaces the pain. Pain not tied
  to a moment is background noise, not a purchase decision.
- **What** — the outcome they're trying to reach or the harm they're trying
  to avoid.
- **How you'd notice it's real** — the observable signals (chapter 03): prior
  spend, a specific recent story, a quantified cost, a hacky workaround.

A solution hypothesis has a different anatomy:

- **Shape** — what the artifact is (a service, a marketplace, a piece of
  software, a template).
- **Mechanism** — the causal claim: *because* our thing does X, the buyer's
  outcome improves by Y.
- **Wedge** — the first, narrow use case you'd sell into.
- **Business model** — who pays, how, and how much.

The two are related but independent. A real problem can survive many wrong
solutions; the right solution to a fake problem is worth zero.

## A worked example (from the module exemplar)

- **Problem hypothesis (testable):** *"First-time technical founders in the
  six months after incorporation experience simultaneous, unordered exposure
  to fundraising, corporate law, equity, and GTM decisions, and lack a
  stage-appropriate map of what to learn when. We'd notice it's real if they
  can tell us a specific recent story of that overwhelm, have already spent
  time or money trying to cope, and can name a concrete cost (a lost deal, a
  dilutive SAFE they didn't understand, a partnership pursued too long)."*
- **Solution hypothesis (out of scope for the first sprint):** *"…therefore
  we'd sell them a staged, dependency-ordered curriculum priced at $X, sold
  as a cohort."*

Discovery tests the first paragraph. The second paragraph gets **ranked**
against alternatives *only after* the first survives contact with the
segment.

## The trap: reversing the order

The common failure mode is running discovery in the shape of solution
validation:

- Show a mockup on the first call. The interview becomes a demo review.
- Lead with "would you use a tool that…?" The buyer answers as a friend, not
  a customer.
- Count "excited responses" as evidence. Excitement is free; commitment is
  the signal.

If your calendar this week is full of solution demos and your problem
hypothesis is not written down as four labelled parts above, you are about
to spend a quarter learning the wrong thing.

## Summary

- Discovery tests the **problem** hypothesis, not the solution.
- A problem hypothesis is well-formed only if it names a **who**, a **when**,
  an **outcome**, and the **signals** you'd use to know it's real.
- Solution hypotheses are worth writing down, but they wait — you rank
  solution shapes *after* the problem has held up.
- Skipping this order is how founders raise a round on flattery and then
  spend it building software nobody was going to buy.
