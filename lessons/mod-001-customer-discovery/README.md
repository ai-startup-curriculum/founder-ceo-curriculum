# Module 001 — Customer Discovery & Idea Validation

> **Stage:** IDEA → PRE-SEED · **Pillar:** Product / Discovery · **Prereqs:** none
> (start here after `startup-foundations`)

## Why this is module one

Every downstream module — modeling, fundraising, sales — is a bet on a belief
about a customer. Customer discovery is where you find out whether that belief
survives contact with reality *before* you spend 18 months and other people's
money on it. The single most expensive founder mistake is building something
nobody urgently needs. This module exists to make that mistake cheap and early.

The goal of discovery is **not** to validate your idea. It's to *find the truth*
about a problem. Those are different jobs, and confusing them is how founders
interview their way into false confidence.

## Learning objectives

After this module you can:

1. Distinguish a **problem hypothesis** from a **solution hypothesis**, and know
   which one discovery tests first.
2. Recruit and run **customer interviews** that surface behavior and evidence,
   not politeness and speculation.
3. Apply the **Mom Test** discipline: ask about the past, not the future; ask
   about their life, not your idea.
4. Synthesize a set of interviews into an **insight memo** that states, with
   evidence, whether a real, urgent, wallet-backed problem exists.
5. Make a defensible **go / pivot / kill** call and know what evidence would
   change it.

## Core concepts

### 1. Problem before solution

You have two hypotheses. A *problem hypothesis* ("early-stage founders can't tell
which of the 200 things pulling at them actually matters right now") and a
*solution hypothesis* ("...so they'd pay for a staged, dependency-ordered
curriculum"). Discovery tests the **problem** first. If the problem isn't real,
urgent, and expensive to the customer, the solution is irrelevant.

### 2. The Mom Test

Named for the rule that you should be able to ask your questions even to your mom
without getting a flattering lie. Three habits:

- **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The moment you pitch, you stop
  learning and start collecting compliments.
- **Ask about specific past behavior, not hypothetical futures.** "What did you
  do the last time this happened?" beats "Would you use a tool that…?" People
  predict their future selves badly and generously.
- **Dig for the evidence behind emotion.** "That's so frustrating" → "Tell me
  about the last time. What did it cost you? What did you try? What did you spend?"

### 3. Signals that a problem is real

- The customer has **already tried to solve it** (hacks, spreadsheets, a competitor,
  hiring someone). Money or time already spent is the strongest signal.
- They can tell you a **specific recent story**, with detail and emotion.
- The problem has a **cost** they can quantify or feel.
- Absence of these = a "nice to have." Nice-to-haves don't fund companies.

### 4. How many, and who

Aim for **~15–20 interviews** in a focused segment. Fewer than ~10 and you're
reacting to noise; you'll usually hear the dominant patterns by ~12–15. Recruit a
**narrow segment** first — "seed-stage technical founders who raised in the last
12 months," not "founders." Narrow segments give sharp signal; broad ones give mush.

### 5. Synthesis > collection

Interviews are worthless un-synthesized. After each, capture: the person, the
specific stories, what they've tried, quantified pain, and surprises. Across the
set, look for **repeated patterns**, not your favorite quote. The deliverable is a
memo that a skeptical co-founder or investor would find honest.
