# Chapter 05 — The Insight Memo: Counted Patterns, Not a Favourite Quote

> **Reads with:** module objective 5 — *synthesise a set of interviews into
> an insight memo that a skeptical co-founder or investor would find honest,
> with counted patterns rather than a favourite quote.*

## Why the memo, and why now

Sixteen interview notes on your laptop are worth less than they feel. Until
they are synthesised into a single document, they are a private impression
in your head, easy to selectively remember, and impossible to defend to
anyone who wasn't in the calls with you. The **insight memo** is the
artifact that turns interviews into a decision.

Two audiences read the memo:

- **You, in three months.** Founder memory reshapes stories to fit whatever
  you've since built. The memo is a time capsule of what the segment
  actually said, in the words they used, before your product opinions
  colonised the notes.
- **A skeptical co-founder or investor**, right now. The memo is the
  document that either lets them agree with your go/pivot/kill call
  (chapter 06) or point to exactly which evidence is missing. If it can't
  do that, it's a story, not an insight memo.

## The shape of a defensible memo

A memo that survives a skeptical read has six parts, and roughly the same
short shape regardless of segment or industry:

1. **Header.** Segment, sprint dates, interview count, and the specific
   problem hypothesis under test (from chapter 01). One paragraph.
2. **What the evidence said.** The counted patterns: named, numbered, and
   ranked by how many interviews independently produced each. Two to four
   patterns is typical; more than five and you probably haven't synthesised.
3. **Surprises and disconfirming evidence.** The interviews that broke the
   pattern, off-segment findings, and anything the sprint discovered by
   accident. Explicitly a section, not a footnote.
4. **Decision.** Go / pivot / kill, in one sentence, with the reasoning
   traceable to the counted patterns.
5. **What would change the call.** The observable evidence — from more
   interviews, a follow-up sprint, or a small experiment — that would flip
   or downgrade the decision.
6. **Method note.** Anything about the sprint that a reader should know:
   segment slippage, sample-size caveats, which patterns came from
   leading-adjacent questions.

That's it. A first insight memo can be one page; a thorough one three or
four. Longer memos rarely improve the decision — they usually indicate the
author is still reasoning at the interview level rather than the pattern
level.

## Counted patterns beat favourite quotes

The single most common failure of a synthesis is what you might call
**cherry-picked-quote syndrome**: the memo leads with the one buyer who
said the most quotable thing, and the reader (including the author) treats
that quote as if it were representative. It isn't. It's an anecdote. A
skeptic can find you a matching quote for the opposite conclusion inside
your own notes.

The corrective is to lead with **counts**. Every claim about the segment
gets a count of the interviews that support it, expressed as a fraction of
the total. The exemplar memo shows the shape:

> **Pattern 1 — The firehose is real and near-universal (14/16).**
> Unprompted, 14 of 16 described month one as "everything at once with no
> order." Representative: *"…"*

The quote comes *after* the count, as illustration, not as evidence. The
number carries the argument.

Two disciplines are needed to produce counts you can trust:

- **Count only unprompted patterns.** If a pattern only appeared after you
  led the buyer to it ("do you feel overwhelmed by...?"), it's your
  hypothesis reflected back at you, not their independent report. Mark
  those separately; they may still be useful, but they don't count as
  discovery.
- **Count against the specific-behavior threshold.** A pattern counts for
  a given interview if the buyer produced at least one of the four signals
  from chapter 03 in support of it — a story, prior spend, a quantified
  cost, or a workaround. Vague agreement does not count.

## The four-signal audit per pattern

For each pattern you identify, walk through the four signals from chapter
03 and note which are present, across how many interviews:

- Prior spend: how many buyers had already paid something to cope?
- Specific recent story: how many produced an episode from the last few
  weeks, with detail?
- Quantified cost: how many could name a concrete cost, in money, time,
  headcount, or opportunity?
- Existing workaround: how many were already running an ugly workaround
  for the pattern?

A pattern with three or four signals present in the majority of interviews
that voted for it is a **strong** pattern. A pattern with only story and
agreement, no spend and no workaround, is a **weak** pattern — worth
noting, not worth deciding on.

This audit is what makes the memo defensible. It replaces "everyone
agreed" (which is unfalsifiable) with "12 of 16 named prior spend, 9 of
16 quantified the cost, 11 of 16 described a workaround" (which is
either true or checkable).

## The surprises section: not optional

The most valuable single practice for producing a memo a skeptic will
trust is a real, prominent **surprises and disconfirming evidence**
section. Two kinds of surprises belong in it:

- **Pattern-breakers.** Buyers inside the segment who did not report the
  pain. Their explanation is a hypothesis about what makes the difference —
  usually a segmentation axis you didn't consider (in the exemplar, "the
  two who didn't feel overwhelmed were second-time founders" reshaped the
  segment to *first-time* technical founders).
- **Unrelated pain.** Things the buyers mentioned repeatedly that were
  *not* the pain you asked about. These often name the real problem you
  should be testing next.

The trap the surprises section is designed to catch is *confirmation
capture*: the tendency, after 15 interviews, to have written the memo in
your head after interview 3 and to have then heard the rest through that
frame. Naming the surprises explicitly forces you to notice them.

## Anonymisation and quoting

Practical hygiene: mask names in the memo (segment fit, not identity, is
what carries the argument), and treat direct quotes as illustrative rather
than load-bearing. A memo that reads like "here are three patterns, with
counts and one quote each" is stronger than one that reads like "here are
seven quotes I liked." When in doubt, remove a quote and see if the
paragraph still makes the case; if it does, the paragraph was doing the
work, not the quote.

## The one-hour drill

The synthesis pass, done right, is about an hour of focused work at the
end of a discovery sprint, in this order:

1. **Sort notes by segment fit.** Set aside off-segment interviews for a
   separate paragraph; you'll count patterns only inside the segment.
2. **Tag each note with candidate patterns.** Read through the notes once,
   marking each with 1–3 short pattern labels (e.g. "firehose,"
   "ordering," "prior spend on advisors"). This is coding in the
   qualitative-research sense — informal but explicit.
3. **Merge and count.** Consolidate near-duplicate labels; count how many
   interviews (unprompted) voted for each; rank by count.
4. **Signal-audit the top patterns.** For each of the top 3–5, tally the
   four signals across the votes. Discard or downgrade any that fail the
   signal check.
5. **Write the memo.** Header, patterns with counts, surprises, decision,
   what would change it, method note.

If step 5 is easy, the earlier steps did their job. If step 5 is where
you're inventing the patterns from scratch, you skipped step 2.

## Summary

- The memo is the artifact that turns a stack of interview notes into a
  decision the segment can be held to and a skeptic can inspect.
- Its shape is short and standard: header, counted patterns, surprises,
  decision, what would change it, method note.
- Every pattern gets a count; every count is of *unprompted* interviews;
  every pattern is signal-audited against the four signals from chapter
  03.
- Surprises are a required section, not an afterthought — they are how you
  catch confirmation capture.
- One or two focused hours after the last interview is usually enough; if
  the memo is hard to write, the interviews weren't captured well.
