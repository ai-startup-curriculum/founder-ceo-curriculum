# Chapter 06 — The Go / Pivot / Kill Call and Its Disconfirming Evidence

> **Reads with:** module objective 6 — *make a defensible go / pivot / kill
> call and name the disconfirming evidence that would change it.*

## What the decision is (and isn't)

The end of a discovery sprint is a decision, not a conclusion. The choices
are three:

- **Go.** The problem is real, urgent, and paid for by an identifiable
  segment. Proceed to design the solution and the first-customer path.
- **Pivot.** The problem shape you tested was partially right — a different
  segment, a different framing of the same pain, or a different trigger
  produces the signal. Redefine and run a targeted follow-up sprint.
- **Kill.** The signals were weak or absent across the segment. Park the
  hypothesis. Move to the next candidate problem on the list.

The framing that matters: **this is a decision about the next four to twelve
weeks of your calendar and burn**, not a permanent verdict on the idea.
"Kill" means you stop spending founder time on this hypothesis this quarter;
it does not mean the underlying idea is proven wrong forever. "Go" means you
commit the next block of capital and attention; it does not mean the
solution shape (chapter 01) is validated — only the problem is.

## The evidence bar for each call

The memo (chapter 05) is the input. The question the decision asks of it is:
*is the pattern strong enough — in count, in signals present, in
consistency inside the segment — to justify the next commitment?*

Rough shape of what each call typically requires:

- **Go.** A majority of the segment (roughly two-thirds or more of ~15–20
  interviews) independently surfaces the pain, and at least three of the
  four chapter-03 signals appear in that majority. Prior spend is present
  in a clear majority — this is the load-bearing signal for "someone is
  already buying a version of this." Surprises exist but do not invalidate
  the core pattern.
- **Pivot.** The pain shows up strongly in a *sub-set* of the segment —
  a recognisable slice defined by a segmentation axis you didn't originally
  filter on (in the exemplar: first-time vs. second-time founders). The
  pivot is *not* "we'll change the product"; it is "we'll re-run discovery
  against a redefined segment or a resharpened problem framing," and the
  memo names both.
- **Kill.** No signal majority, prior spend rare or absent, workarounds
  absent, and buyers polite but non-committal. Alternatively: the pain is
  real but small — buyers can name it but don't rank it in their top
  three problems — meaning it's a nice-to-have.

A call that doesn't fit any of these is usually a *sample-size or
segmenting problem*, not a decision. If the interviews are too few or the
segment is too heterogeneous to see a pattern either way, the honest
decision is a **fifth option no memo should call anything else**: *"run
one more sprint before deciding."* Give it a scope and a deadline in the
memo.

## Why the call must be written down

Founders often make an implicit decision after a sprint — the discovery
"felt good" or "felt weak" — and act on the impression without ever
writing it down. This is the failure mode that makes discovery sprints
unaccountable. Three costs follow:

- **No calibration.** If the call isn't written, the founder can't look
  back six months later and check whether the call was right. The main
  way founders get better at discovery is by comparing memos to what
  actually happened; without written calls, there's nothing to compare.
- **No alignment.** Co-founders and early advisors form their own
  interpretations of a shared sprint. If the call isn't written, the team
  is running on three slightly different theories of what happens next.
- **No falsifiability.** An implicit decision is unfalsifiable because
  nobody remembers exactly what it was.

The rule is straightforward: **the memo ends in one sentence naming the
call**, and everything downstream — the next roadmap, the next hire, the
next fundraise story — is measured against it.

## Naming the disconfirming evidence

The most important paragraph of the memo, for the credibility of every
paragraph before it, is the one that names *what would change the call*.
It is the discipline that separates a discovery memo from a rationalisation.

For a **Go**, name the observation that would flip you to pivot or kill:

- *"If prior-spend had been rare across the set (fewer than a quarter of
  interviews), this would drop to a nice-to-have → kill."*
- *"If the follow-up sprint with the next segment doesn't reproduce the
  pattern, we down-grade to a pivot."*
- *"If the first three sales conversations get stuck at 'not urgent this
  quarter,' we treat the discovery signal as over-interpreted."*

For a **Pivot**, name the observation that would return you to Go or drop
to Kill:

- *"If the resegmented sprint reproduces the pattern at the same strength
  (10+/15 with prior spend), we treat it as Go on the new segment."*
- *"If it doesn't, we kill this hypothesis rather than pivot again."*

For a **Kill**, name the observation that would revive it:

- *"If a specific market event (e.g., a regulatory change, a new large
  buyer entering) reshapes the buyer's urgency, we re-open."*
- *"If we independently see three of the segment's buyers spending on
  someone else's product in the same shape within a quarter, that's a
  demand signal we missed and worth re-testing."*

Naming these evidence rules up front does two things: it forces you to
notice, in the following weeks, when you're seeing them (rather than
retroactively rationalising); and it lets a co-founder or investor hold
you to the call, because you specified in advance what would change it.

## Common failure shapes

Three failure modes are worth naming explicitly because they occur
routinely and each looks like a legitimate call from inside the founder's
head.

- **"Everyone loved it — go!"** The memo counts enthusiasm rather than
  signals. Compliments are not evidence (chapter 02); a Go supported by
  enthusiasm without prior spend, workarounds, or quantified cost is a
  Go on politeness.
- **"It's not quite there, but we'll figure it out during build."** The
  founder can't name a pattern strong enough for Go and doesn't want to
  Kill, so the decision is left implicit and the build starts anyway. The
  operative words are "figure it out" — which is a phrase that means
  "no memo." Force the pivot or the kill; do not build past an unclear
  discovery.
- **"The interviews were mixed; I'll do more."** Sometimes true. Often
  the shape of a founder unwilling to accept a Kill. The tell is that
  the next round of interviews is loosely scoped and the segment quietly
  widens (chapter 04). If you're planning another round, name what it's
  testing that this one didn't, and what a Kill result would look like.

## From the call to the next block of work

Once the call is written and its disconfirming evidence named, the memo
should hand off cleanly to what happens next:

- **Go** hands off to mod-002 (canvas and unit-economics) — the problem is
  real, now model the business around it — and to the first-solution
  sketch. It also hands off to the ongoing-discovery discipline that
  belongs to the CPO track (chapter 07).
- **Pivot** hands off to a scoped follow-up sprint (fresh segment or
  reframed problem), *not* to product design.
- **Kill** hands off to the next candidate problem on the founder's list.
  Nothing else changes — no build starts, no round is raised on a killed
  hypothesis. The kill is the deliverable.

## Summary

- The decision is one of three: **go**, **pivot**, or **kill**. A written
  memo ends in one sentence naming which.
- Each call has a rough evidence bar tied to the counted patterns and
  signals from chapters 03 and 05.
- Naming the **disconfirming evidence** — the observation that would flip
  the call — is what makes the memo a discipline rather than a rationalisation.
- Three failure shapes are worth avoiding by name: enthusiasm read as
  evidence, ambiguous "figure it out" builds, and quiet extension of the
  sprint to avoid a kill.
- The call hands off cleanly to the next block of work; nothing downstream
  should proceed without the call being explicit.
