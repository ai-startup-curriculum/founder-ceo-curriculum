# Exercise 01 — Founder-led Sales Script & Pilot Design

**Module:** 006 Founder-led Sales · **Stage:** SEED · **Time:**
~1 week (≈6–8 focused hours) · **Reads with:** chapters
[01](../01-founder-led-sales-motion.md),
[02](../02-discovery-in-a-sales-context.md),
[03](../03-hard-qualification-and-disqualification.md),
[04](../04-pilot-scope-and-success-metric.md), and
[05](../05-pilot-to-enterprise-contract.md).

## Problem statement

The founder-led sales motion — discovery → qualification →
pilot → contract — is only useful when the founder runs it
against a **real, named prospect**, not a hypothetical one.
This exercise builds the four founder-authored artifacts you
need before you take the first sales call for that prospect:
a discovery script, a qualification rubric with
disqualification triggers, a pilot design, and a written
path from a successful pilot to a signed contract.

The exercise ends with a short "what a 'no' at each stage
teaches you" note, because the feedback loop from mod-006
back into mod-001 discovery and mod-002 pricing (chapter 06)
starts with knowing what each type of loss actually means.

## Deliverables (four founder artifacts, plus one call)

1. **A founder-led sales script for one real prospect** — the
   discovery call guide (5–8 open, past-tense, non-leading
   questions per chapter 02), the qualification criteria you'll
   apply during and after the call (MEDDPICC or BANT+C per
   chapter 03), and the disqualification criteria (who is *not*
   a fit) that let you kill a bad-fit deal fast.
2. **A pilot design** — scope, the single pre-agreed
   quantitative success metric, duration (4–8 weeks), price
   (paid or free-with-teeth), signers on both sides, and the
   exit criteria that turn a successful pilot into a signed
   contract (per chapter 04).
3. **A path from successful pilot to signed contract** — the
   five parallel-but-sequenced tracks from chapter 05
   (commercial proposal / order form, security review, DPA/BAA,
   MSA, procurement + signature), each with a named owner on
   the buyer side and an expected calendar.
4. **A one-paragraph "what a 'no' at each stage teaches you"
   note** — for each of the four stages (discovery,
   qualification, pilot, contract), one sentence naming the
   most likely reason for a "no" at that stage and what that
   "no" tells you about your segment, your value proposition,
   or your pricing (per chapter 06's feedback loop).

> Depends on mod-002: the value proposition, segment, and
> pricing you sell against here are the ones you modeled on
> the lean canvas. If any of those changed while running this
> exercise, update the canvas — that's the discovery signal
> doing its job (chapter 06).

## Scenario

Pick one:

- **Your real startup** (recommended if you're an operating
  founder): pick **one** named prospect on your current
  pipeline — a real company, a real buyer, and a real
  discovery call you can book this week. Use your actual
  segment definition and value proposition from mod-002.
- **Simulated:** you're selling the mod-002 exemplar product
  (a staged, dependency-ordered founder curriculum) to a
  **named accelerator or founder-support program** that could
  adopt it as a cohort resource. The buyer is the program
  director; the economic buyer may be the same person or a
  managing partner one level up. Use the segment and price
  point from the mod-002 exemplar; if the exemplar isn't
  authored yet, use $99/mo/founder or a $10k/cohort license
  and defend it in the pilot design.

## Steps

1. **Name one prospect.** Company, buyer (title + name if you
   have it), how you got the intro (or how you'll get it this
   week), and the one sentence that says why *this* prospect
   fits *your* segment definition from mod-002. If you can't
   fill this in for a real company, the segment is too vague
   — go back to mod-001 discovery notes and sharpen it before
   you write the script.
2. **Write the discovery guide.** 5–8 open, past-tense
   questions built on the Mom Test discipline from
   [chapter 02](../02-discovery-in-a-sales-context.md).
   Every question must pass the Mom Test: about the buyer's
   life and past behavior, never about your product, never
   hypothetical. Include at least one "walk me through the
   last time you [tried to solve this]" and one "what have
   you already tried / bought / built?" The five-move
   structure (frame → last-time → already-tried →
   cost-and-decision-process → trigger-and-next-step) is a
   good scaffold; ~30 minutes total.
3. **Write the qualification criteria.** Pick MEDDPICC or
   BANT+C from [chapter 03](../03-hard-qualification-and-disqualification.md)
   and use it explicitly. MEDDPICC (Metrics, Economic buyer,
   Decision criteria, Decision process, Paper process,
   Identify pain, Champion, Competition) is the standard
   enterprise qualifier and forces you to name the eight
   things a deal needs to close. Use BANT+C for smaller,
   single-stakeholder deals; note which framework and why.
4. **Write the disqualification criteria.** For each
   qualification dimension, name the observation during the
   call that would move you to *walk away this week*. "No
   named economic buyer after three calls" is a disqualifier.
   "The champion can't articulate the pain in their own
   words" is a disqualifier. Founder-led sales fails when
   founders chase bad-fit deals for months because they
   cannot afford to say no to a possible logo; the fix is
   to write the "no" rule before the call, not after the
   pilot is halfway through (chapter 03).
5. **Design the pilot.** Four required fields plus three
   for a clean end, from
   [chapter 04](../04-pilot-scope-and-success-metric.md):
   scope (one workflow, one team, one metric, with an
   explicit out-of-scope list), success metric (pre-agreed,
   quantitative, owned by their side, with baseline and
   target), duration (4–8 weeks, calendar-fixed with a
   named end date), price (paid best, free-with-teeth
   second, free never), exit criteria (success / partial /
   miss), signers (economic buyer, not just champion), and
   attribution (their data, their measurement, their
   reporter).
6. **Design the path from pilot to contract.** From
   [chapter 05](../05-pilot-to-enterprise-contract.md),
   list the five tracks (commercial proposal / order form,
   security review, DPA/BAA, MSA, procurement + signature),
   each with the buyer-side owner and an expected calendar.
   Plan to the midpoint of realistic ranges: 30–60 days for
   small mid-market, 60–90 for standard mid-market, 90–180
   for enterprise. Note which of your papers (MSA, DPA, BAA,
   order form, security package) are ready, drafted, or not
   yet.
7. **Rehearse the discovery call.** Read the guide aloud, in
   order, to a co-founder or an operator friend playing the
   buyer. Time it. Cut questions until you can finish in
   30 minutes with 10 minutes left for their questions. Any
   question that pulled a compliment or a hypothetical answer
   needs to be rewritten in the past tense — the Mom Test
   rule applies to founders selling as much as to founders
   discovering (chapter 02).
8. **Write the "what a 'no' teaches you" note.** For each of
   the four stages, one sentence: what's the most likely
   reason for the "no" there, and what does that "no" tell
   you? Chapter 06 covers the feedback loop; the short
   version: "no" at discovery usually means the segment or
   problem framing is wrong (go back to mod-001). "No" at
   qualification usually means there's no economic buyer or
   no budget cycle (segment or pricing). "No" at the pilot
   usually means the success metric didn't move enough
   (product or scope). "No" at contract usually means
   procurement, security, or a competing priority killed a
   deal you'd already won.

## Discovery script template (fill inline)

```
Prospect:
  Company:                ___
  Buyer (title, name):    ___
  Economic buyer (if diff): ___
  Intro source / how you got the meeting: ___
  Why this prospect fits the mod-002 segment (one sentence): ___

Discovery guide (5–8 questions; past-tense, non-leading; ~30 min):
  1. Walk me through the last time you tried to [problem area]. What happened?
  2. What have you already tried / bought / built to solve this?
  3. Who else was involved / affected by that? Who signed off on the spend?
  4. What did it cost you (time, money, headcount) — roughly?
  5. What triggered you to take that call with me / look at this again now?
  6. If nothing changes, what does this look like in 6 months?
  7. Who else on your team would need to be part of a decision on this?
  8. What would have to be true for you to move on this in the next quarter?

Mom Test self-check (mark each question):
  [ ] About their life / past behavior, not about my product
  [ ] Past-tense, not hypothetical ("would you use...?")
  [ ] Open-ended (no yes/no)
  [ ] Would still make sense if the buyer had never heard of us
```

## Acceptance criteria

You are done when *all* of the following are true:

- [ ] One named prospect: company, buyer (title + name),
      intro source, one-sentence segment-fit statement.
- [ ] Discovery guide has 5–8 questions, every one Mom-Test
      clean (past-tense, non-leading, non-product), and
      rehearsed to fit in 30 minutes with time to spare.
- [ ] Qualification framework named (MEDDPICC or BANT+C),
      with every dimension either filled with a specific
      answer or marked TBD with a plan to fill it in the
      first 1–2 calls.
- [ ] Disqualification criteria include at least 3
      pre-committed walk-away triggers, each observable
      during discovery or the next 1–2 calls.
- [ ] Pilot design has all four required fields (scope,
      metric, duration, price) plus the three end-clean
      fields (exit criteria, signers, attribution). Success
      metric is pre-agreed, quantitative, and owned by their
      side, with baseline and target both stated.
- [ ] Pilot-to-contract path names the five tracks, each
      with a buyer-side owner and an expected calendar.
      Papers on your side are marked ready / drafted / not
      yet honestly.
- [ ] "What a 'no' teaches you" note is filled for all four
      stages, with each entry naming which artifact
      upstream (mod-001 discovery, mod-002 canvas, pilot
      template, qualification rubric) would update on that
      loss.

## Rubric (self-assess, then compare to the exemplar)

<!-- needs-research: exemplar for mod-006 not yet authored; rubric-vs-exemplar comparison assumes future exemplars/mod-006-founder-led-sales/. -->

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Prospect specificity | "Companies like X" as a category | One named company, named buyer, real intro path, mod-002 segment fit stated in one sentence |
| Discovery questions | Leading / hypothetical ("Would you use…?") | Past-tense, behavioral, Mom-Test clean; would still make sense if the buyer had never heard of you |
| Qualification | Vibes / "they seemed excited" | Named framework (MEDDPICC or BANT+C) with every dimension answered in the buyer's own words |
| Disqualification | Implicit / never written down | Pre-committed walk-away criteria; you can point to the one that would fire |
| Pilot scope | "Pilot the product" | One workflow, one team, one metric; out-of-scope items explicitly listed |
| Pilot success metric | "They liked it" / qualitative | Pre-agreed, quantitative, baseline + target, owned by the buyer's side |
| Pilot pricing | Free-forever pilot with no conversion criteria | Paid or free-with-teeth: signed pilot agreement, named economic buyer, pre-agreed conversion criteria |
| Pilot signers | Only the champion signs | Economic buyer signs on their side; founder signs on ours |
| Pilot → contract path | "Then we send a proposal" | Five tracks named, buyer-side owner and calendar for each, papers marked honestly |
| "No" learning | "We'll figure it out" | Per-stage "no" hypothesis with the specific upstream artifact (mod-001 / mod-002 / template) that updates |

## Definition of done

A skeptical co-founder or an operator who has closed
enterprise deals before would read your script,
qualification criteria, pilot design, and pilot-to-contract
path and agree that (a) the discovery guide is Mom-Test
clean and would actually surface whether the pain is real,
(b) the qualification and disqualification criteria are
specific enough that a bad-fit deal gets killed inside two
calls, not two quarters, (c) the pilot has a pre-agreed,
quantitative success metric and a named path to a signed
contract that the buyer's side — not just yours — could
recite from memory, and (d) each "no" outcome maps to a
specific upstream artifact that will change if that "no"
happens.
