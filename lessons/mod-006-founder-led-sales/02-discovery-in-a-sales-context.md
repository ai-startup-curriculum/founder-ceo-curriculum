# Chapter 02 — Discovery in a Sales Context: The Mom Test, Continued

> **Reads with:** module objective 2 — *design a discovery call
> that continues the mod-001 method into a sales context — ask
> about the last time the pain happened, what they tried, what
> it cost — rather than pitching the product.*

## Discovery does not stop at the sale

Mod-001 taught the discovery interview: open, past-tense,
non-leading questions about the buyer's life and past behavior,
never about your product. The single reference for the method
is Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013) — the discipline is
named after the observation that even your own mother will lie
to you about your business idea if you ask her leading
questions.
<!-- needs-research: Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*, 2013 — momtestbook.com. -->

The mistake founders make once they cross into sales is to
assume the method was for the pre-product phase and now the
job is to pitch. It is not. **The discovery call in a sales
context is still a discovery call.** The only thing that has
changed is that the buyer might, if the pain is real and you
are a fit, become a customer. The questions do not change.

Two reasons the discipline persists into sales:

- **The buyer will tell you what to sell if you ask.** The
  words they use for the pain are the words that should be in
  the next outbound email. The tools they tried and abandoned
  are the tools you position against. The person who signed
  off on the last spend is the economic buyer for yours. None
  of that shows up if you spend the call pitching.
- **The pitch is a mirror, not a monologue.** The strongest
  founder-led sales calls end with the buyer articulating,
  in their own words, why the product would work for them —
  because the founder heard, restated, and pointed at fit,
  not because the founder delivered a deck. That only
  happens if discovery ran first.

## The three questions every discovery call must answer

A well-run discovery call answers three questions the founder
did not know the answer to going in:

1. **Is the pain real, and how recent was the last time it
   hurt?** Not "would this be useful," which any polite
   respondent answers yes to. The load-bearing question is
   *when did this last happen and what did it cost you?*
2. **What have they already done about it?** Every buyer who
   has real pain has tried something. Homegrown tools,
   competitor products, consultants, a manual process, an
   unhappy internal customer. The list of what they tried is
   the map of your competitive landscape at that account.
3. **Who has the authority — and the budget cycle — to fix
   it?** The person on the call is often not the economic
   buyer. Discovering the economic buyer, the budget cycle,
   and the decision process is discovery work, not
   qualification-later work. If you leave the call not
   knowing who signs, you have to run another one.

Everything else — company overview, current stack, team size,
strategic priorities — is context that supports these three,
not a substitute for them.

## The Mom Test rules, translated into sales

The four Mom Test rules from mod-001, restated for a sales
call where the buyer knows you are trying to sell them
something:

- **Talk about their life, not your product.** "Walk me
  through the last time [problem X] happened at your team"
  beats "does our product's feature Y sound useful?"
- **Ask about specific past behavior, not hypothetical
  future behavior.** "What did you do the last time this
  came up?" beats "would you use a tool that…?" Hypothetical
  answers are cheap for the buyer to give and worthless to
  you.
- **Talk less. Listen more.** The 70/30 rule (buyer talks
  70%, you talk 30%) is a floor, not a ceiling. On the best
  discovery calls, the buyer talks 80–90% of the time.
- **Reactions and compliments are not commitments.** "This
  is really interesting" is not a signal. A next meeting
  with a named additional attendee on their side, with a
  specific artifact you will bring, on a specific date, is
  a signal. Chapter 03 covers the distinction more sharply.

## A discovery call that works, in five moves

A 30-minute discovery call has time for roughly five moves.
Each one has a purpose; each one respects the rules above.

**Move 1 — Frame (2 min).** State why you are on the call and
what you are hoping to learn. Ask for permission to ask
questions. *"I'm hoping to understand how your team runs
[workflow area] today. I'm going to ask a bunch of questions
about your current process; is that okay to start with?"* This
sets the discovery frame explicitly so the buyer does not
expect a demo.

**Move 2 — The last-time question (8 min).** *"Walk me through
the last time you tried to solve [problem area]. What
happened?"* This is the load-bearing question. Follow up on
whatever they say with "and then what happened?" until the
story is complete. You are listening for: how recent, how
painful, who was involved, what it cost, what they tried.

**Move 3 — The already-tried question (6 min).** *"What have
you already tried, bought, or built to solve this?"* This maps
the competitive landscape at the account. Every item they
name gets a follow-up: *"How did that work out?"* You are
listening for: which categories they've bracketed, which
vendors they've evaluated, what internal tooling exists.

**Move 4 — The cost and decision-process question (8 min).**
*"Roughly, what has this cost you — time, money, headcount?
Who signed off on the last spend in this area, and what did
that process look like?"* You are listening for: quantified
pain, the economic buyer, the budget cycle, the procurement
pattern.

**Move 5 — The trigger and next-step question (6 min).**
*"What triggered you to take this call now? And what would
have to be true for you to move on this in the next quarter?"*
You are listening for: the compelling event (or its absence),
the timeline, the decision criteria. Close by proposing a
specific next step — usually a follow-up meeting with a named
additional attendee — with a date and an artifact.

Notice that no move includes a pitch or a demo. If the buyer
asks for one during moves 2–4, defer: *"Happy to — can we
finish the current thread first, then I'll show you specifically
where we might fit?"* If the buyer asks for one during move 5,
schedule the demo as the next step with the additional
attendee they name. This trades a small amount of first-call
excitement for a much better second call.

## Questions to have ready (and the ones to avoid)

These pass the Mom Test:

- "Walk me through the last time [X] happened."
- "What did you do about it?"
- "What have you already tried / bought / built for this?"
- "What did that cost you — time, money, or headcount?"
- "Who else was involved? Who signed off on the spend?"
- "What triggered you to look at this again now?"
- "If nothing changes, what does this look like in 6 months?"
- "Who else on your team would need to be part of a decision
  on this?"
- "What would have to be true for you to move on this in the
  next quarter?"

These do not:

- "Would you use a tool that…?" (hypothetical)
- "Do you like the idea of…?" (compliment-fishing)
- "How much would you pay for…?" (asks the buyer to price
  your product; they can't)
- "Does that make sense?" (yes/no, and asks the buyer to
  agree with you rather than share information)
- "What features would you want?" (delegates product
  design to a single buyer with no context on your other
  buyers)

## Note-taking discipline

Discovery is worthless if it does not persist to a place the
founder actually re-reads. The minimum note structure per
call:

```
Account:                  ___
Contact (name, title):    ___
Date:                     ___

Their words for the pain (verbatim, not paraphrased):
  ___

The last-time story (dates, people, cost):
  ___

What they've tried:
  ___

Economic buyer (named or unknown):    ___
Budget cycle / timeline (named or unknown): ___
Trigger event (named or unknown):     ___

Next step (with date + owner):        ___

Signals I heard for fit / disfit:      ___
```

Verbatim buyer language is more valuable than your paraphrase.
"We're drowning in support tickets that could've been prevented"
is a headline for the next outbound campaign; "customer
support was a pain point" is not.

## When discovery reveals the deal isn't there

The most valuable outcome of a discovery call is not always a
next meeting. It is sometimes the founder recognising, in the
call, that the pain is real but distant, the buyer is not the
economic buyer, or there is no budget cycle to hit this year —
and having the discipline to disqualify then rather than three
calls later.

That is not a failed call. That is discovery doing its job:
saving the founder six weeks of chasing a deal that was never
going to close, so the founder can spend those six weeks on
a deal that is. Chapter 03 covers how to write the
disqualification rules that make that decision easier to
make in the moment.

## Feeding discovery back into the canvas

Every discovery call, whether it advances or not, produces
inputs for the artifacts from mod-001 (discovery synthesis)
and mod-002 (lean canvas, unit economics). At minimum, weekly:

- Update the segment definition on the canvas if the calls
  are surfacing a subsegment that fits better.
- Update the problem statement using the buyer's own words if
  the words on the canvas are yours rather than theirs.
- Note the tools/vendors they mention as "already tried" —
  those become your competitive landscape.
- Note the prices, budgets, or ROI numbers they cite —
  those calibrate the mod-002 pricing assumption.

Chapter 06 covers this feedback loop in detail; the point
here is that discovery is not a phase you exit — it is a
practice you keep running, and the sales calls are some of
your best-attended discovery sessions.

## Summary

- Discovery in a sales context is **still discovery**. The
  Mom Test rules from mod-001 apply unchanged; the only
  addition is that the buyer might become a customer.
- Every discovery call must answer three questions: **is the
  pain real and how recent, what have they already tried,
  and who has the authority and budget cycle to fix it.**
- A working 30-minute call structure: **frame → last-time
  question → already-tried question → cost and decision-
  process question → trigger and next-step question.** No
  pitch. No demo. A specific next step.
- **Verbatim buyer language** is more valuable than your
  paraphrase. Note it, re-read it, and let it rewrite your
  outbound and your canvas.
- The most valuable outcome of a call is sometimes to
  **disqualify then** rather than three calls later. Chapter
  03 covers the disqualification rules.
