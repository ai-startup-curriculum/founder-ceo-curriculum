# Exercise 01 — Founder-led Sales Script & Pilot Design

**Module:** 006 Founder-led Sales · **Stage:** SEED · **Time:** ~1 week
(≈6–8 focused hours)

## Deliverables (three founder artifacts, plus one call)

1. **A founder-led sales script for one real prospect** — the discovery call
   guide (5–8 open, past-tense, non-leading questions), the qualification
   criteria you'll apply during and after the call, and the disqualification
   criteria (who is *not* a fit) that let you kill a bad-fit deal fast.
2. **A pilot design** — scope (what you will and will not do), the single
   pre-agreed success metric, duration, price (paid or free), and the exit
   criteria that turn a successful pilot into a signed contract.
3. **A one-paragraph "what a 'no' at each stage teaches you" note** — for
   each of the four stages (discovery, qualification, pilot, contract), one
   sentence naming the most likely reason for a "no" at that stage, and
   what that "no" tells you about your segment, your value proposition,
   or your pricing.

> Depends on mod-002: the value proposition, segment, and pricing you sell
> against here are the ones you modeled on the lean canvas. If any of those
> changed while running this exercise, update the canvas — that's the
> discovery signal doing its job.

## Scenario

Pick one:

- **Your real startup** (recommended if you're an operating founder): pick
  **one** named prospect on your current pipeline — a real company, a real
  buyer, and a real discovery call you can book this week. Use your actual
  segment definition and value proposition from mod-002.
- **Simulated:** you're selling the mod-002 exemplar product (a staged,
  dependency-ordered founder curriculum) to a **named accelerator or
  founder-support program** that could adopt it as a cohort resource. The
  buyer is the program director; the economic buyer may be the same
  person or a managing partner one level up. Use the segment and price
  point from the mod-002 exemplar; if the exemplar isn't authored yet,
  use $99/mo/founder or a $10k/cohort license and defend it in the pilot
  design.

## Steps

1. **Name one prospect.** Company, buyer (title + name if you have it),
   how you got the intro (or how you'll get it this week), and the one
   sentence that says why *this* prospect fits *your* segment definition
   from mod-002. If you can't fill this in for a real company, the
   segment is too vague — go back to mod-001 discovery notes and sharpen
   it before you write the script.
2. **Write the discovery guide.** 5–8 open, past-tense questions. Every
   question must pass the Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*,
   2013): about the buyer's life and past behavior, never about your
   product, never hypothetical. Include at least one "walk me through the
   last time you [tried to solve this]" and one "what have you already
   tried / bought / built?" Founder-led discovery calls exist to learn
   whether the pain is real and quantified — not to pitch.
3. **Write the qualification criteria.** Pick a named framework and use
   it explicitly. **MEDDPICC** — Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision
   criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion, Paper process,
   Competition — is the standard enterprise-sales qualifier and forces
   you to name the six things a deal needs to close. If you're selling
   into smaller organizations where MEDDPICC is overkill, use
   **BANT** (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline) but note that BANT
   under-weights the champion, which is often the single most
   deal-critical role at seed stage.
   <!-- needs-research: MEDDIC / MEDDPICC has no single canonical published source; commonly attributed to Jack Napoli and colleagues at PTC in the 1990s. -->
4. **Write the disqualification criteria.** For each qualification
   dimension, name the observation during the call that would move you
   to *walk away this week*. "No named economic buyer after three calls"
   is a disqualifier. "The champion can't articulate the pain in their
   own words" is a disqualifier. Founder-led sales fails when founders
   chase bad-fit deals for months because they cannot afford to say no
   to a possible logo; the fix is to write the "no" rule before the call,
   not after the pilot is halfway through.
5. **Design the pilot.** Four fields: scope, success metric, duration,
   price. The pilot exists to answer one question: *does this
   customer's world change measurably when they use our product?* So the
   success metric is pre-agreed and quantitative — not "they liked it."
   Duration is short enough that a "no" doesn't drown you (4–8 weeks is
   typical for enterprise B2B software). Price is either **paid** (best,
   because the buyer's finance team has now touched the deal and you
   have a first invoice) or **free-with-teeth** (a signed pilot
   agreement, a named economic buyer, and pre-agreed conversion
   criteria). Scope is deliberately narrow — one workflow, one team,
   one metric.
6. **Design the path from pilot to contract.** Write the exit criteria:
   what result of the pilot triggers the commercial conversation? Who
   signs the contract on their side (economic buyer, procurement,
   security, legal)? What is the standard MSA / order form you will
   send? What is the shortest plausible calendar from "pilot succeeded"
   to "signature"? At seed stage, a 30–60 day pilot-to-close window is
   realistic for a mid-market buyer; longer for large enterprise, where
   procurement and security reviews alone can be 60–90 days.
   <!-- needs-research: mid-market vs enterprise procurement-cycle length; commonly cited ranges, no single authoritative source. -->
7. **Rehearse the discovery call.** Read the guide aloud, in order,
   to a co-founder or an operator friend playing the buyer. Time it.
   Cut questions until you can finish in 30 minutes with 10 minutes
   left for their questions. Any question that pulled a compliment or a
   hypothetical answer needs to be rewritten in the past tense — the
   Mom Test rule applies to founders selling as much as to founders
   discovering.
8. **Write the "what a 'no' teaches you" note.** For each of the four
   stages, one sentence: what's the most likely reason for the "no"
   there, and what does that "no" tell you? "No" at discovery usually
   means the segment or the problem framing is wrong (go back to
   mod-001). "No" at qualification usually means there's no economic
   buyer or no budget cycle you can hit (segment or pricing). "No" at
   the pilot usually means the success metric didn't move, or moved
   less than the buyer needs (product or scope). "No" at contract
   usually means procurement, security, or a competing internal
   priority killed a deal you'd already won — the fix is to surface
   those risks earlier next time.

## Discovery script template (fill inline)

```
Prospect:
  Company:                ___
  Buyer (title, name):    ___
  Economic buyer (if diff): ___
  Intro source / how you got the meeting: ___
  Why this prospect fits the mod-002 segment (one sentence): ___

Discovery guide (5–8 questions; past-tense, non-leading; ~30 min):
  1. Walk me through the last time you tried to [problem area]. What happened?
  2. What have you already tried / bought / built to solve this?
  3. Who else was involved / affected by that? Who signed off on the spend?
  4. What did it cost you (time, money, headcount) — roughly?
  5. What triggered you to take that call with me / look at this again now?
  6. If nothing changes, what does this look like in 6 months?
  7. Who else on your team would need to be part of a decision on this?
  8. What would have to be true for you to move on this in the next quarter?

Mom Test self-check (mark each question):
  [ ] About their life / past behavior, not about my product
  [ ] Past-tense, not hypothetical ("would you use...?")
  [ ] Open-ended (no yes/no)
  [ ] Would still make sense if the buyer had never heard of us
```

## Rubric (self-assess, then compare to the exemplar)

<!-- needs-research: exemplar for mod-006 not yet authored; rubric-vs-exemplar comparison assumes future exemplars/mod-006-founder-led-sales/. -->

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Prospect specificity | "Companies like X" as a category | One named company, named buyer, real intro path, mod-002 segment fit stated in one sentence |
| Discovery questions | Leading / hypothetical ("Would you use…?") | Past-tense, behavioral, Mom-Test clean; would still make sense if the buyer had never heard of you |
| Qualification | Vibes / "they seemed excited" | Named framework (MEDDPICC or BANT) with every dimension answered in the buyer's own words |
| Disqualification | Implicit / never written down | Pre-committed walk-away criteria; you can point to the one that would fire |
| Pilot scope | "Pilot the product" | One workflow, one team, one metric; out-of-scope items explicitly listed |
| Pilot success metric | "They liked it" / qualitative | Pre-agreed, quantitative, baseline + target, owned by the buyer's side |
| Pilot pricing | Free-forever pilot with no conversion criteria | Paid or free-with-teeth: signed pilot agreement, named economic buyer, pre-agreed conversion criteria |
| Pilot → contract path | "Then we send a proposal" | Stepwise path with security / procurement / DPA / signer named and calendared |
| "No" learning | "We'll figure it out" | Per-stage "no" hypothesis with what it changes upstream (segment, canvas, pricing) |

## Definition of done

A skeptical co-founder or an operator who has closed enterprise deals
before would read your script, qualification criteria, and pilot design
and agree that (a) the discovery guide is Mom-Test clean and would
actually surface whether the pain is real, (b) the qualification and
disqualification criteria are specific enough that a bad-fit deal gets
killed inside two calls, not two quarters, and (c) the pilot has a
pre-agreed, quantitative success metric and a named path to a signed
contract that the buyer's side — not just yours — could recite from
memory.
