# Chapter 03 — Hard Qualification and Disqualification: Write the "No" Rule Before the Call

> **Reads with:** module objective 3 — *qualify hard: define who
> is not a fit and disqualify them explicitly, using segment-fit
> signals from mod-002 rather than optimism.*

## Qualification is a decision, not a vibe

Qualification is the founder's answer to a single question,
made about every open opportunity, on a written rubric:
*is this a real deal worth spending founder time on?* The
answer is either yes (advance to pilot) or no (walk away this
week). There is no third answer that isn't a soft no dressed
as a maybe.

Founder-led sales fails, at the pipeline level, when founders
substitute vibes for qualification. Symptoms:

- The pipeline slide shows twenty logos, ten of which have
  not moved in six weeks.
- When asked "what has to happen for [account] to close,"
  the founder gives a generic answer ("they need to see
  more value") rather than a named person, action, and date.
- Deals that are "just about to close" have been "just about
  to close" for three quarterly board updates.
- The founder is emotionally invested in the biggest logo,
  which happens to be the one with no economic buyer.

The fix is a written qualification framework applied
consistently. Two are standard.

## MEDDPICC: the enterprise qualifier

**MEDDPICC** is the enterprise-sales qualification standard.
It is commonly attributed to Jack Napoli and colleagues at
PTC in the 1990s and evolved into MEDDPICC over the following
decades. The value is the discipline of naming, in the buyer's
own words, the six (or eight) things a deal needs to close.
<!-- needs-research: MEDDIC / MEDDPICC has no single canonical published source; commonly attributed to Jack Napoli and colleagues at PTC in the 1990s. Force Management, MEDDIC Academy, and Andy Whyte's *MEDDICC* (2020) are common secondary anchors. -->

```
M — Metrics              What quantified pain or gain will we measure?
E — Economic buyer       Who has the budget authority to sign?
D — Decision criteria    What will they use to compare us vs. alternatives?
D — Decision process     What are the actual steps + calendar from yes to signature?
P — Paper process        MSA? Order form? Security review? DPA? Procurement portal?
I — Identify pain        In their own words, what's broken today?
C — Champion             Who inside will spend political capital to close this?
C — Competition          What's the incumbent / status quo / other vendor?
```

The rule for MEDDPICC to be worth doing: **every field is
filled with a named person, a named process, a named number,
or a specific artifact — not "TBD" and not a generic
placeholder.** "Metrics: they'll save time" is not a metric.
"Metrics: reduce time-to-resolution on P1 tickets from
current baseline of 4.2 hours to under 2 hours, measured by
their existing Zendesk instance, reported weekly by their
Head of Support" is a metric.

Reading the fields as a checklist rather than a set of things
to *fill in*, the deal that lacks any of them is not qualified:

- **No Metrics** → no way to score the pilot; deal fails at
  pilot exit.
- **No Economic buyer** → no one on their side can sign; deal
  stalls at contract.
- **No Decision criteria** → the buyer will invent them
  under pressure at the end, usually to your disadvantage.
- **No Decision process** → you cannot plan the calendar;
  deals slip indefinitely.
- **No Paper process** → procurement and security surface
  90 days late, unplanned.
- **No Identified pain** → the deal is aspirational; will
  lose to "do nothing."
- **No Champion** → nobody on their side is pushing when
  attention drifts to other priorities.
- **No Competition analysis** → you'll be surprised by an
  incumbent workaround in the last week.

## BANT: the SMB qualifier

**BANT** — Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline — is the older,
lighter qualifier, credited to IBM in the 1960s and still
useful for smaller organizations where MEDDPICC is overkill.
<!-- needs-research: BANT is widely attributed to IBM sales training in the 1960s but has no single canonical published source. HubSpot, Gartner, and Salesforce publish common secondary treatments. -->

```
B — Budget      Is there budget for a purchase in this category?
A — Authority   Is the person you are talking to the decision-maker?
N — Need        Is there a real, articulated need?
T — Timeline    Is there a specific timeline to buy?
```

BANT's under-weight is that it does not name a **champion**.
At seed stage, the champion — the internal advocate who will
spend political capital when your deal competes with other
internal priorities — is often the single most deal-critical
role. Even if you use BANT for its brevity, add a champion
field: BANT + C.

The practical rule: use MEDDPICC for any deal with a $50k+
annual contract value or with more than three stakeholders on
the buyer side; use BANT (+C) for smaller, single-stakeholder
deals. Do not use both on the same deal; pick one and be
consistent so the pipeline is comparable.

## The champion, specifically

Every early-stage deal that closes has a champion — sometimes
more than one, but at least one — and every stalled deal
either never had one or lost the one it had. The champion is
not the economic buyer; the champion is the person who *cares
whether this deal closes* and will do the internal work to
make it happen.

Champion diagnostics — asked of yourself, about each deal:

- Can they articulate the pain and the proposed solution in
  their own words, without prompting from you?
- Have they introduced you to at least one other person
  internally who matters (their manager, a peer stakeholder,
  procurement)?
- Have they done a similar procurement (any vendor, any
  category) in the last 12–18 months, so they know how the
  process works internally?
- Do they respond to your emails inside 48 hours during
  active discovery?
- If you go quiet for two weeks, do *they* reach out?

Two-out-of-five is a coach, not a champion. Four-out-of-five
is a champion. Five-out-of-five and you probably have a real
deal. Deals with no champion — no matter how enthusiastic the
first call was — are not deals; they are learning.

## Disqualification: write the rule before the call

Qualification's opposite number is **disqualification** — the
explicit walk-away rule for each dimension. The discipline is
to write the rule *before* the call, not after the pilot is
halfway through, because the psychology of a live opportunity
is exactly wrong for making the walk-away decision cleanly.

A working disqualification rubric names, for each MEDDPICC
dimension, the specific observation that triggers "walk away
this week":

```
Trigger observation                                    → Walk away because
------------------------------------------------------ → -----------------
No named economic buyer after N calls (N = 3)          → Deal has no exit
Champion cannot articulate the pain unprompted         → Deal has no push
No budget cycle we can hit this fiscal year            → Deal can't close
Success metric is unmeasurable or unowned              → Pilot can't pass
Procurement or security review makes the pilot
  uneconomic (e.g., 6-month security review for a
  4-week pilot)                                        → Timing kills deal
Buyer wants a custom product build as a
  prerequisite                                         → Not a deal;
                                                         consulting engagement
Incumbent contract renews in 8+ months and buyer
  cannot commit to switching mid-term                  → Wait, don't chase
```

The founder's own list will differ. Two design rules:

- **Every rule must be observable during discovery or the
  next 1–2 calls**, not something you learn at pilot exit.
- **Every rule must be prescriptive**: "walk away *this
  week*," not "reconsider fit." The point is to remove the
  founder's optimism from the decision.

The best qualification rubrics grow one rule at a time,
after each lost deal, from the pattern that killed it. The
lost deal cost you three months; the new rule saves you three
months on the next deal that looks like it.

## Using mod-002 segment-fit signals

Mod-002's lean canvas defines a **segment** — the target
customer type this business exists to serve. Every
qualification rubric should include a segment-fit filter that
is answered before MEDDPICC/BANT is even opened:

- Does this account match the segment we defined on the
  canvas (industry, size band, technology stack, buying
  motion, urgency profile)?
- If not, is the mismatch a signal we should update the
  canvas — or a signal to walk away?

Founders who skip the segment-fit check end up with a
pipeline of "interesting" logos that do not resemble each
other. Interesting logos are how sales orgs get lost in the
desert. A pipeline where every won deal looks like every
other won deal is how a repeatable playbook eventually
emerges — which is the point of the whole motion (chapter
07).

A useful discipline: for each won deal and each lost deal,
write one sentence answering "did this look like our segment?"
When the answer to "won" is consistently "yes" and the answer
to "lost" is consistently "no, we chased it anyway," the
segment definition is doing its job. When "lost" is
consistently "yes, and I don't know why we lost," go back to
discovery — the segment is right, the value prop or the
delivery is off.

## Optimism as an enemy of qualification

Founder-led sales has a specific psychological failure mode:
the founder wants each deal to be real because closing it
would validate the company, so the founder unconsciously
soft-pedals the disqualification signals in the very deals
that most need to be disqualified.

Countermeasures that actually work:

- **Have a co-founder or investor run a pipeline review
  weekly.** They read the deal notes and ask "what has
  to be true for this to close by [date]?" for each open
  deal. Their answers filter your optimism.
- **Kill one deal per week, out loud.** Naming a deal you
  are walking away from — and telling the buyer politely
  that this doesn't seem like the right fit for either
  side right now — is a muscle. It gets stronger with
  reps. The alternative is a pipeline full of ghosts.
- **Prefer a small qualified pipeline to a large
  unqualified one.** Ten deals with full MEDDPICC fills
  produce more revenue than fifty deals with vibes. The
  founder's calendar is the scarce resource; treat it
  like one.

## The output of qualification

A qualified deal is one where the founder can, without notes,
recite:

- The buyer's own words for the pain.
- The named economic buyer, the champion, and their
  relationship.
- The next specific step, with a date and an owner.
- The paper process (MSA, DPA, security questionnaire,
  procurement portal) and roughly how long it will take.
- What has to happen this month for the deal to close in the
  next quarter.

If any of those is missing, the deal is not yet qualified —
which is not a problem, it just means the next founder action
on that deal is more discovery, not more pitching. Chapter 04
covers what happens next: designing a pilot that turns a
qualified deal into a signed contract.

## Summary

- **Qualification is a decision on a written rubric**, made
  weekly on every open opportunity. The answer is "advance"
  or "walk away," not "keep it warm."
- **MEDDPICC** is the enterprise-sales standard: Metrics,
  Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Paper
  process, Identify pain, Champion, Competition. Every field
  is filled with a name or a number or the deal isn't
  qualified yet.
- **BANT (+C)** is the SMB qualifier — Budget, Authority,
  Need, Timeline, plus Champion. Pick one framework per
  deal and apply it consistently.
- The **champion** — not the economic buyer — is the
  person who cares whether the deal closes. Deals without
  champions do not close.
- **Disqualification rules are written before the call**,
  because live-opportunity psychology is exactly wrong for
  cleanly deciding to walk away.
- The **mod-002 segment definition** is a pre-filter for
  every qualification. Interesting logos outside the segment
  are how sales orgs get lost in the desert.
