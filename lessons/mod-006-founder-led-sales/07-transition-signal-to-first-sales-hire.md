# Chapter 07 — The Transition Signal: When to Hire the First SDR/AE

> **Reads with:** module objective 7 — *recognise the transition
> signal: when the founder-led motion has produced ~$500k–$2M
> ARR across ~5–15 named customers with a repeatable playbook,
> it is time to hire the first SDR / AE — this module ends there.*

## The two symmetrical mistakes

There are two common mistakes at the transition from
founder-led sales to a first sales hire, and they are
mirror images of each other:

- **Hire too early.** The founder is tired of sales, the
  playbook is still incomplete, and the fantasy of "if I
  just hire someone, deals will keep closing without me"
  wins over the reality that the deals were closing
  because the founder was in the room. The AE, no matter
  how talented, cannot replicate what has not been
  documented. Six months later, ARR growth has stalled,
  the AE is being blamed for missed quota, and the founder
  is back in every deal but now also managing an
  unhappy employee.
- **Hire too late.** The playbook is working. Deals are
  repeatable. The founder is still the only closer, which
  means the company grows at the rate of the founder's
  personal calendar. The founder-CEO's other work — product,
  strategy, hiring, the board — starves. A competitor with
  a working sales team out-executes on the ground the
  founder proved.

The transition signal in this chapter exists to name the
condition under which the first symmetrical mistake becomes
less costly than the second. It is not a single number; it
is a small set of conditions that together define
"repeatable enough to delegate."

## The signal, in three parts

The transition condition is that **all three** of the
following are true, not just one:

1. **~$500k–$2M ARR** across 5–15 named customers.
2. **A written playbook** covering discovery, qualification,
   pilot design, and contract path.
3. **A repeatable pattern**: the last 3–5 closed deals look
   like each other — same segment, same buyer type, same
   pilot template, same objections, same price band, same
   sales-cycle length.

Missing any one of the three, and the transition is
premature.

### Part 1: $500k–$2M ARR across 5–15 named customers

The ARR band is not a hard rule; it is a signal of
market feedback. Below $500k, the sample is too small
to distinguish "we can sell it" from "we got lucky
three times." Above $2M, the founder is usually
already past the point of being able to run every
deal personally without other work suffering.

The **customer count** matters as much as the ARR.
Five to fifteen customers is a large enough sample to
see a pattern, small enough that each customer is a
named account the founder can debrief in detail. One
$1M customer is not the same signal as ten $100k
customers; the ten teach you a repeatable motion, the
one teaches you how to serve one customer well.

Two failure patterns the customer count catches:

- **One-whale ARR.** $1.5M ARR concentrated in one or
  two customers. The founder has proven they can close
  one big deal, not that they have a repeatable
  motion. Hire sales when you can prove the second and
  third deal both closed on the same playbook, not
  when the first one lands.
- **Long-tail low-ACV ARR.** $500k ARR spread across
  200 customers at $2.5k each. The playbook the
  founder ran is either self-service that never needed
  a founder, or a poorly-fit segment where the AE
  economics don't work. Different signal, different
  next hire (product growth or marketing, not AE).

The band that supports an AE hire is closer to
**5–15 customers with ACVs in a range where an AE can
cover 15–30 accounts as a book of business** — typically
$25k–$250k ACV. Outside that band, the transition is
different work.

### Part 2: A written playbook

The playbook is a document (spreadsheet, Notion page,
Google Doc, wiki — pick one, keep it maintained). At
minimum, it covers:

- **Ideal customer profile (ICP).** Segment, size band,
  buyer title, buyer trigger events, sales-cycle length,
  price band, expected pilot design. Derived from the
  won-deals cohort, not from a wish list.
- **Discovery script.** The 5–8 Mom-Test questions the
  founder actually asks, in the order that has produced
  the best conversion. Reworked from mod-001's discovery
  guide + chapter 02 here.
- **Qualification rubric.** MEDDPICC (or BANT+C) with the
  disqualification triggers written in from chapter 03.
- **Pilot template.** The 2-page pilot agreement from
  chapter 04, with the success-metric families the
  founder has seen work in this segment.
- **Objection responses.** For each of the top 5–10
  objections in the log from chapter 06, the founder's
  actual response, and the outcome that response tends to
  produce.
- **Contract path.** Standard MSA, DPA, order form,
  pricing sheet, and the calendar template for
  procurement / security / signature.
- **Case studies.** 3–5 won-customer stories (with the
  customer's permission) that the AE can cite in early
  discovery calls to build credibility.

The playbook is what the AE inherits on their first day.
Without it, the AE has to run the founder-led motion
themselves from scratch — which they cannot do, because
they lack the founder's authority to bend deals and
change the roadmap.

A useful smoke test: **could a skeptical AE, handed the
playbook and no other conversation with the founder,
close the next deal in the pipeline?** If yes, the
playbook is ready. If not, the founder still has work to
capture what only lives in their head.

### Part 3: A repeatable pattern in the last 3–5 deals

The last 3–5 closed deals should look like each other on
five dimensions:

- **Segment.** Same industry, same size band, same use
  case, same team the product goes into.
- **Buyer type.** Same title/level, same relationship to
  the pain, same procurement pattern.
- **Pilot template.** Same success-metric family, same
  duration, same paid/free structure.
- **Objection pattern.** The same 2–3 objections show up
  and the founder's responses to them work reliably.
- **Sales-cycle length.** Pilot-to-signature calendar is
  within ~30% of the median across the cohort.

When the last 3–5 deals look like each other on those
dimensions, the founder has found a **wedge** — a
specific slice of the market where the product, the
buyer, and the price all line up. That wedge is what an
AE can be hired to work.

When the last 3–5 deals *don't* look like each other,
the company has not yet found the wedge. Each deal is
its own snowflake. Hiring an AE at that point is
premature; the founder needs 2–4 more months of
running the motion until a pattern emerges.

## Two negative signals that mean "not yet"

Even when ARR and customer count are in the band, two
signals tell you to keep running the motion yourself
for another quarter:

- **The founder cannot state the playbook in one
  sitting.** If the founder cannot, in one meeting,
  hand off the ICP, script, qualification rubric,
  pilot template, and contract path to a hypothetical
  AE without drawing on 15+ deals worth of situational
  memory, the motion isn't packaged yet. Package it,
  then hire.
- **The win rate at each stage varies wildly by
  founder mood or opponent.** If the same objection
  the founder responds to differently every time is
  producing wildly different outcomes, the play is not
  yet a play. It is craft. Craft doesn't hand off; the
  founder has to reduce it to a rule.

## Choosing the first hire: SDR vs AE

Once the transition signal fires, the first sales hire is
one of two roles:

- **First AE (Account Executive).** A quota-carrying seller
  who owns deals end-to-end: qualifies, runs pilots, closes
  contracts. Hire an AE first when: the sales-cycle length
  is > 60 days, the ACV is > $25k, the pilot involves
  hands-on collaboration, and the founder's calendar is
  bottlenecking on running deals rather than sourcing
  them.
- **First SDR (Sales Development Rep).** A pipeline-
  building role that qualifies inbound leads or does
  outbound prospecting, handing warm-enough conversations
  to the founder (or later, the first AE). Hire an SDR
  first when: the founder is bottlenecked on *pipeline
  volume* (not enough opportunities to work), the sales-
  cycle length is short, or the deal size supports SDR
  economics without an AE layer.

At seed stage, most B2B SaaS companies with enterprise
ACVs hire an AE first — the deals are complex enough that
handing them off to a specialist closer produces more
leverage than adding pipeline volume the founder can't
close.

## What the founder still does after the first sales hire

The transition is not a full exit from sales. Post-hire,
the founder still:

- **Runs the top 1–3 strategic deals personally.** Deals
  with lighthouse-account potential, deals with unusual
  product asks, deals that will define the next segment.
- **Personally references-sells** to prospects the AE is
  closing — a 30-minute call with the founder is the
  fastest way to close the top of the AE's pipeline.
- **Owns the pricing model.** Concessions above a
  threshold, custom deal structures, unusual terms all
  route to the founder. The mod-002 unit economics update
  from chapter 06 stays the founder's job.
- **Debriefs every won and lost deal for the first
  6 months of the AE's tenure.** Not to second-guess the
  AE; to make sure the signal that used to reach the
  founder directly is still reaching them through the
  first-hire filter.
- **Recruits AE hire #2 and #3 personally.** The founder
  is the person who best understands the profile that
  works for this specific motion; that judgment doesn't
  transfer to the first AE for at least a year.

The transition ends the *motion*; it does not end the
founder's ownership of the *judgment* about the motion.

## The exit criterion for this module

You have finished this module — and are ready for the
`startup-product-gtm-curriculum` (level 30) — when:

- You have run the founder-led motion personally for at
  least ~$500k of ARR across 5+ named customers.
- The playbook is written and would let a hypothetical AE
  close the next deal without you.
- The last 3–5 deals look like each other on segment,
  buyer, pilot, objections, cycle length.
- You can name the specific first sales hire (SDR or AE),
  the profile you'll hire against, and the first-90-day
  ramp plan.

Below that bar, this module's content is the current work.
Above it, the material in this module remains true — the
Mom Test discipline, the qualification rubric, the pilot
design, the paper process, the feedback loop — but the
operating pattern they live inside is different, and the
peer track teaches what to do next.

## Summary

- Two symmetrical mistakes: **hire sales too early** (the
  AE cannot replicate what wasn't documented) or **too
  late** (the company grows at the founder's calendar). The
  transition signal names when the first mistake is less
  costly than the second.
- The signal is **three conditions, all true**: ~$500k–$2M
  ARR across 5–15 named customers, a written playbook
  covering the full motion, and a repeatable pattern in the
  last 3–5 deals on segment / buyer / pilot / objection /
  cycle-length.
- **Two negative signals mean "not yet"**: the founder
  cannot state the playbook in one sitting, or wins-per-
  stage vary wildly with the founder in the room.
- The **first hire is an AE for complex enterprise deals**
  (long cycle, high ACV, hands-on pilots) or **an SDR for
  pipeline-volume bottlenecks** (short cycle, ACV supports
  the layer). Most seed-stage B2B SaaS starts with an AE.
- Post-transition, the founder still runs **strategic
  deals, references-sells for AE deals, owns pricing,
  debriefs every deal for 6 months, and recruits AEs #2
  and #3 personally.** The transition ends the motion,
  not the judgment.
- **This module ends at the transition signal.** The peer
  track (`startup-product-gtm-curriculum`, level 30) owns
  what comes next.
